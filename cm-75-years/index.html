So what happened?

And the political history, it's more layered than the diaspora version you've probably been handed. So let me walk you through it. The Abolition of Old Medicine proposal, drafted by Yu Yunxiu, was carried by the first Central Board of Health conference on 25 February 1929, the one the Ministry of Health convened, and it was carried in amended form, as principles for registering old-style physicians (《規定舊醫登記案原則》), basically, a resolution that merged four separate proposals aimed at old medicine. It never became law. There was a real organized fight. Practitioners went out into the street, and on 17 March 1929 a national delegates' congress convened in Shanghai, which is the date, still, the date the profession keeps as National Medicine Day (國醫節). The proposal got shelved before it ever became national policy, right? The medicine survived. Barely.

Years later, the communists won the civil war, and at first they did something closer to Yu's programme than to its opposite. The First National Health Conference in 1950 set the policy of uniting Chinese and Western medicine, and in the same breath it set up improvement schools (中醫進修學校), to retrain Chinese-medicine doctors in Western medicine. Seventeen of those schools by the end of 1951, and a hundred and one improvement classes under various names alongside them. Union on those terms means one side doing the learning. It's the same way a merger works when one company keeps the name and the other one keeps nothing, right? The full-throated promotion comes later, after Mao's criticism of the Health Ministry in 1954, and the removal of its leadership in 1955. Kim Taylor lays that sequence out and I'm following her on it. Mao didn't speak at that conference. His contribution was a written inscription calling for the unity. The line everybody quotes at you is a different one, and I'll be straight that the year on it is unsettled. Taylor prints it, Slate dates it to 1950, and the PRC's own compilations put this wording in 1953 and 1954. Nothing I'm arguing turns on which. Here it is. "Our nation's health work teams are large. They have to concern themselves with over 500 million people [including the] young, old, and ill. ... At present, doctors of Western medicine are few, and thus the broad masses of the people, and in particular the peasants, rely on Chinese medicine to treat illness. Therefore, we must strive for the complete unification of Chinese medicine." That's Kim Taylor's translation. Five hundred million people, right? So that's a logistics number, and not a love letter. He isn't praising the medicine, he's counting the doctors. Scaling Western medicine for 500 million people, that was never going to happen, honestly, not on post-civil-war resources. And promoting TCM worked as anti-imperialist signaling on top of that, indigenous Chinese knowledge standing up against Western medical dominance. It wasn't an ideological commitment to the tradition.

And what's more screwed up? Mao himself didn't believe in it. His personal physician, Li Zhisui, recounts him saying so directly in The Private Life of Chairman Mao. "Even though I believe we should promote Chinese medicine, I personally do not believe in it. I don't take Chinese medicine." That quote is challenged on its sourcing, and I've put the challenge in the reading list for you.

What the Cultural Revolution cost

Then the Cultural Revolution kicked off in 1966, and you got the Four Olds campaign. Old Customs, Old Culture, Old Habits, Old Ideas. And a lot of Chinese medicine got swept into it. It's in Miriam Gross's 2018 paper in Medical History, and the next three sentences are hers. Ancient medical tomes burned. Family grave sites and lineage halls destroyed, right? And an extraordinary number of the elite elderly doctors, who were the actual repositories of the tradition, persecuted and tortured and in some cases killed outright. Gross went through a commemorative collection of biographies, forty-six elite physicians in Jilin Province, basically, physicians who were alive during the Cultural Revolution, and she found that 63% of them recorded experiences from it, experiences that led to retirement, or to devastating illness, or to death. Nationally, the number of Chinese medicine doctors went from 361,000 in 1959, down to 240,000 by 1977. Now the objection, and it's the first one anybody raises. Wasn't the same decade expanding Chinese medicine? It was. Gross's own paper gives you roughly 1.08 million barefoot doctors between 1969 and mid-1974, a national herbal campaign, and an acupuncture revival that turned up large numbers of new points. So the decade didn't simply destroy this medicine. It destroyed the elite, lineage-transmitted, text-based end of it while mass-producing a thin standardised version at village level, and the number I just quoted counts the first kind of practitioner and not everybody holding a needle. So sit with what that means. The medicine the state was officially promoting, it was getting its infrastructure dismantled at the same time, and I mean the people and the books and the family trees that connected the two of them, all three going at once. And think of it as a trade where the manual was never really written down, because the manual was a person. You can lose a lot of copies of a book. You cannot photocopy a grandmother.

After it wound down in the late 70s, the lineages that survived had to rebuild out of whatever pieces were still lying around. And this next bit is lineage talk, not history. Yu Yunxiu was right about the description

Come back to 1929. Yu Yunxiu was not a fool, and he was not a foreign agent, right? He was a Chinese physician, Japanese-trained, and he had read the classics closely enough to write a book, attacking them line by line. He looked at yin-yang, at the five phases, at the channels. And he said, this is a cosmology, it's groundless, and as long as people think this way, the country cannot have modern public health. So abolish the whole thing.

Here's the sentence everything turns on. 舊醫一日不除，民眾思想一日不變，新醫事業一日不向上，衛生行政一日不能進展. So long as old medicine is not abolished, the thinking of the people will not change, the enterprise of new medicine will not advance, and health administration cannot progress. And the measures underneath it were not gentle. One-off registration and no more after that. Retraining. Time-limited licences for the older practitioners. No advertising. Close the schools. So he went after the trade as well, and I'm not going to pretend he didn't. Think of it as going after the licence and the language in one motion, right, and only one of those two was the point. But look at what he put at the top of the demand. Not the needle, not the decoction, not the pulse or any of the rest of the trade. The thinking. He'll concede clinical results in places, and he does. What he wants gone is the way of thinking, and he says so in the sentence he chose to hand the measures off with.

Now take me, ninety-seven years later, looking at the same yin-yang and the same five phases and the same channels you were taught. This is a cosmology, right? It runs on correspondence and resonance and worked timing and a conditioned operator, right? And there's a word for systems, systems built that way.

So he and I agree on the description. We disagree completely on the verdict, and that's the whole thing, that's what the rest of this is about. Want to dig into the actual record

The apparatus for this chapter. The categories, the anthropology, the character work and the dating of the nine operations travel with chapter two and chapter three, where they're argued.

On what a curriculum transmits versus what a lineage does. This is the one I'd hand you first. Elisabeth Hsu, The Transmission of Chinese Medicine (Cambridge University Press, 1999), is fieldwork on three modes of transmission, inside one Chinese city, the standardized college, personal transmission from a senior doctor, and secret transmission, including cultivation practice. So that's your difference between a syllabus and a lineage, documented by somebody with no stake at all in my argument.

The Dongui Bogam. Compiled 1610, published 1613, and it does preserve passages from earlier Chinese texts, ones later lost in China. That preservation happened at compilation, and the book circulated in Chinese printings continuously afterwards, so it's evidence of Korean transmission holding material China lost, and not of a text China went and retrieved after the Cultural Revolution. Academic accounts of the post-1976 reconstruction emphasize domestic recovery more than the diaspora-lineage version does. Korea is the only leg of this I can support; there is no evidence I can find for a Japanese Kampo or Vietnamese contribution to the reconstruction, and I make no claim about either.

On Bill 88. Read the bill as introduced on 28 February 2022, and not the one that passed, because they're different documents and this is the one place a reader can catch me looking wrong when I'm not. The Oversight Authority and the voluntary registration aren't in the schedule text; that's the Ministry of Health's stated destination, on the record through the Canadian Press on 2 March 2022. At clause-by-clause in the Standing Committee on Social Policy on 9 March the government gave notice it would vote against its own schedule, and the College's announcement of 14 March, "Bill 88 Amended to Remove TCM Repeal Act, 2022", dates the removal to 10 March.

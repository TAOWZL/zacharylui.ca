Map one: the channels as rivers

The Ling Shu is the second half of the Yellow Emperor's classic. Chapter 12 of it is a treatise called Jing Shui (經水), the channel waters, and it takes the twelve channels of the body and pairs each one with an actual waterway on the map of ancient China. It opens with Huang Di putting it to Qi Bo, and this is Unschuld's rendering. "The twelve conduit/stream vessels link up with the twelve stream waters outside, and they are connected with the five long-term depots and six short-term repositories inside." Rivers outside, organs inside, one plumbing, right? And it's literal in a way class never taught it to me. Not "the body is a bit like a landscape." This channel, that river. Right.

Notice what kind of statement that is. It isn't an observation about the body. It's a correspondence between the body and the empire, asserted as a working fact. Sivin's phrase for the larger pattern is the isomorphism of body, state and cosmos, three things built to the same plan, so what holds in one tells you something about the others. That's not a simile. That's a claim about structure. And it matters clinically, not just as an image, because if the channel is a river then everything you already know about rivers applies to it. A blockage backs water up for miles behind it, and the whole line goes wrong at once. So do you have to wade in at the jam to move the water? You don't. You work the river somewhere you can reach. You open a sluice. You dredge a narrow stretch. You warm a frozen section. And the whole watercourse answers you.

Now, that sluice picture is me extending the doctrine's own water-thinking, and I'm not quoting a chapter at you. The treatise itself uses the river pairings closer to dosage. Needle depth and moxa amounts, keyed to how big and how deep each river runs. But the same water-thinking rides through the rest of the channel doctrine. The five transport points every student memorizes get taught to you through water names. Well, spring, stream, river, sea. And the classic's own gloss on them runs on water verbs, the qi emerging and gliding and pouring as it comes up the limb. So does needling a point at the wrist to move something in your neck sound strange? On the water map it's just how rivers work.

The modern curriculum keeps channel theory. It keeps the pathways, it keeps the distal points. It just tends to teach you the rivers as a memory aid, a picturesque way of saying linear pathway, where in the classical frame the same material reads closer to an engineering manual. Dredging, flooding, dams, seasonal water levels. You want a receipt for that? One, from a mainland classical scholar. Liu Lihong points out that zhi (治), the very word for treatment, is built on the water radical, and that the old methods of regulating water, which are dredging, conducting, drawing, leading, enclosing and storing, are the same family of moves as treating a person. Now, that's an etymology. It hints, it doesn't prove. And technically the water part of the character starts out as a river's name. But the water was in the word long before I showed up.

Map two: the organs as officials

The second map is stranger, and I love it more. Chapter 8 of the Su Wen is the Ling Lan Mi Dian Lun (靈蘭秘典論), usually translated as the Secret Treatise of the Numinous Orchid, and it describes the organs as offices of a royal court. The heart is the sovereign, and clarity of spirit issues from it. The lung is the chancellor. The liver is the general, and planning comes out of it. On it goes, through the whole government. Twelve offices by the text's own count, eleven by mine, because spleen and stomach share a desk and the remaining seat belongs to the centre of the chest, which later tradition reads as the pericardium. Commentators have argued over that count for centuries. Would you do that over a figure of speech? They took the org chart seriously.

So the organs run like a court. Each official has a job, a jurisdiction, and a characteristic way of failing, and that last part is what a parts list can't hold for you. You're being handed a person in a post who gets thwarted. A general whose plans keep getting blocked stops planning and starts seething. Now, Su Wen 8 doesn't actually spell that failure mode out. The thwarted-general picture gets built over later centuries, and the standard periodization runs about like this. Zhu Danxi is the one who really works the stuck-depression idea, and for him it sits mostly in the middle burner. The liver version is Ming and Qing work. Map three: bazi, the calendar underneath

Under the rivers and under the court there's a third map, and it's the one my Canadian curriculum kept quietest about. Time. When you were born. What season you're standing in. What year is doing what to you.

Bazi is your birth chart. Eight characters in four pairs. Year, month, day, hour, and each pair is a stem and a branch. A snapshot of the qi weather you arrived in. And chapter 1 mentioned instructors bringing it into clinical teaching off-syllabus. The classic carries the year-and-season level of the same map, since the Su Wen spends seven of its longest chapters on Wu Yun Liu Qi, the five periods and six qi. And then there's needling by the clock. Zi Wu Liu Zhu picks points by when the channel tide is high. Ling Gui Ba Fa does the same job with the nine-palace numbers layered on. Both of them run the day and the hour through the stems and branches to read off which point is open on you.

That operation is date selection (擇日). It's what you do to pick a wedding day, or a burial day, or a groundbreaking. And I'm not the one who noticed. Lu and Needham put acupuncture time-selection in the almanac tradition in Celestial Lancets, and the day-books and hemerological manuals it comes out of are Marc Kalinowski's territory. Same almanac logic. Same ganzhi cipher. Same output, this hour open and that one shut. The only difference is a needle at the end of the calculation instead of a shovel. So that's divination sitting in the middle of a needling manual, right? And there is nothing in physiology that says a point on your arm is open at 3pm on a jiazi day.

As Zi Wu Liu Zhu gets practised, you consult a chart. That's a lookup you can't derive. Faith with a table in front of it, right? But the actual method is a derivation, and if you refine that derivation far enough the time drops out of it, hour then day then month then year, because the calendar was only ever a proxy for a state. Want to dig into the actual record

The rivers. Paul Unschuld's complete translation of the Huang Di Nei Jing Ling Shu (University of California Press, 2016) has the Jing Shui treatise pairing the twelve channels with the twelve waterways. His Huang Di Nei Jing Su Wen: Nature, Knowledge, Imagery in an Ancient Chinese Medical Text (University of California Press, 2003) is where the water-and-state imagery gets its fullest treatment. For the body/state/cosmos isomorphism, go to Nathan Sivin, "State, Cosmos, and Body in the Last Three Centuries B.C.," Harvard Journal of Asiatic Studies vol. 55, no. 1 (1995), pp. 5-37. That article is superseded by Lloyd and Sivin's The Way and the Word (Yale University Press, 2002).

The court. Unschuld and Hermann Tessenow's Huang Di Nei Jing Su Wen translation (University of California Press, 2011) has chapter 8, 靈蘭秘典論, the Ling Lan Mi Dian Lun. That's where you'll find the twelve offices, the heart as sovereign, the liver as general, and San Jiao as the official in charge of dredging the waterways. The San Jiao form debate runs through Nan Jing 25 and lands at difficulty 38, 有名而無形. Unschuld's Nan-ching: The Classic of Difficult Issues (University of California Press) covers it.

The stagnation periodization. The Zhu Danxi to Ming-Qing to 1950s sequence I give you for liver qi stagnation is the standard textbook account and not one scholar's finding, and I've stated it as such in the chapter.

The calendar. The Wu Yun Liu Qi material sits in the Su Wen's seven great treatises, chapters 66 through 71 plus 74, in the Unschuld and Tessenow translation, along with the scholarship on their later, Tang-era insertion. Now the dating, since the chapter body no longer carries it. The great treatises entered the Su Wen late and scholars read them as a later stratum, and bazi as the full four-pillar art matures around the Tang and Song, which is roughly a millennium after the Nei Jing core. And the needling-by-the-clock manuals are later again. The earliest surviving Zi Wu Liu Zhu monograph is 何若愚, 《子午流注針經》, 1153, annotated by 閻明廣. Ling Gui Ba Fa in worked form is standardly credited to 徐鳳, 《針灸大全》, 1439, which converts the ganzhi to nine-palace numbers, sums them, then divides by 9 on yang days or 6 on yin days. And neither manual was ever examined by anybody, so they are the vivid case and the great treatises are the load-bearing one. Lu Gwei-djen and Joseph Needham's Celestial Lancets: A History and Rationale of Acupuncture and Moxa (Cambridge University Press, 1980) is the standard English treatment of acupuncture and time, and it's where time-selection gets situated in the almanac tradition, with the irony that they were arguing the material was proto-scientific and I'm arguing the opposite. For the 擇日 side, the day-books and the hemerological manuals, Marc Kalinowski is the standard scholar. See Donald Harper and Marc Kalinowski, eds., Books of Fate and Popular Culture in Early China (Brill, 2017). The phrasing about a needle instead of a shovel is mine. The link between medical time-selection and the almanac arts is theirs.

The water in 治. Liu Lihong's Classical Chinese Medicine (Chinese University Press, 2020, edited by Heiner Fruehauf, translated by Weiss, Buchtel, and Wilms) has it in his treatment chapter.

Correspondence in the room. Judith Farquhar, Knowing Practice: The Clinical Encounter of Chinese Medicine (Westview Press, 1994) is the standard ethnography of correspondence reasoning generating treatment decisions instead of ornamenting them, and it's the evidence my composite scenes can't be. Chapter four is where I lean on her hardest.

Channels and points in modern clinical form. Peter Deadman, Mazin Al-Khafaji, and Kevin Baker, A Manual of Acupuncture, is the standard reference, and for the broader entanglement of medicine with the mantic and calendrical arts, the Routledge Handbook of Chinese Medicine (edited by Vivienne Lo and Michael Stanley-Baker, 2022) collects the current scholarship for you.

Saam. This used to be the one tool here I couldn't hand you a clean English monograph for, since the root text is the Saam-doin chimgu yogyeol and the scholarship mostly lives in Korean. That's changed. Essentials of Saam Acupuncture, translated by Philip Suger and edited by Allen Tsaur and Michael Brown, brings Saam's teachings and Jisan's clinical records into English with a hundred and twenty-four cases, and the publisher dates the source material to between 1644 and 1742. Purple Cloud Press, and check the edition year against theirs and not against mine. The press is Johan Hausen's, the same Johan Hausen who told me in chapter two that I had 灸 wrong. I've decided that's funny.

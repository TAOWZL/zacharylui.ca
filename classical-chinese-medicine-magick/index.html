So what is it?

Okay let me back up a second and explain what TCM actually is, because most people don't even know that part.

Most people think Chinese medicine is 4,000 years old. The version you actually run into in a Canadian clinic is more like 75 years old. That's not me being dramatic, that's literally what the curriculum is.

In Canada, the training's two years for acupuncture and three years for traditional Chinese medicine practitioner. The extra year is herbology, single herbs, classical formulas, decoction prep, that whole side of things. Either way, you memorize a lot of stuff. You've got 361 acupoints sitting on the 12 primary channels plus 2 of the 8 extraordinary vessels (the Du Mai and Ren Mai, those carry their own points, the other six don't, you just get basic coverage on those for opening and closing points and pathways and symptomatology). Then 48 extra points, the 奇穴 qíxué, that sit off-channel. Plus the microsystems, auricular, scalp, Korean hand, sometimes facial too. The four examinations of looking, listening, asking, palpation, plus point location, channel theory, yin-yang, five phases, zangfu pattern diagnostics, etiology and pathology. Then clinical subjects, internal medicine, gynecology, pediatrics, dermatology, orthopedics. The modalities, needling, moxibustion, cupping, gua sha, tuina, occasional bone-setting (banfa), TCM food therapy, plus qigong and tai chi as physical education, typically Yang style. Then you do student clinic, somewhere between 540 and 840 hours treating real patients under supervision before they let you graduate.

And acupoints get coded by abbreviation, you'll see things like LI4, ST36, LV3, instead of their pinyin names which would be He Gu, Zu San Li, Tai Chong. That actually matters because the names carry meaning and the codes don't.

Right? That's the official curriculum. Unofficially, faculty bring lineage material into clinical instruction, you'll see things like Master Tung's points, Dr. Richard Tan's Balance Method, Japanese and Korean acupuncture styles, medical qigong, Wu Yun Liu Qi (五运六气 wǔ yùn liù qì) calendrical acupuncture, bazi diagnostics, family-line technique adjustments. Stuff that's not in the CTCMPAO syllabus but every decent instructor brings in anyway.

Intake is the 10 questions formalized by Ming dynasty physician Zhang Jingyue, chills and fever, sweating, head and body, stool and urination, appetite and thirst, chest and abdomen, hearing, sleep, pain, emotions, plus menstruation for women. You also do the biomedical-compatible review (vitals, contraindications, red flags), but that part isn't classical, that's added on for the modern clinic so you don't accidentally kill anybody.

Now this isn't how Chinese medicine always looked, by the way. The version most people meet now is the post-war reform. So mid-20th century, Mao takes over, political turn brings a materialist worldview in, and pretty much everything classical gets pushed out. Daoist ritual, Buddhist ritual, astrology, feng shui, all of it just classified as superstition. Texts were lost or burned. Practitioners got silenced or killed or had to flee the country. The medicine survived at all really because the new system needed to feed millions of people and couldn't really afford to just throw out the herbal pharmacy. So the reformers kept what was biomedical-compatible, cut what wasn't, reformatted it for hospitals and exam boards, and that became the modern profession. Familiar enough to register as medicine but exotic enough to still file as alternative. Kind of like the panda, which is technically a bear but gets marketed as something else.

The classical material didn't disappear though. Practitioners with lineage transmission migrated to Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, North America, Europe, kept teaching the older material outside the mainland framework. Rural pockets in Yunnan and Sichuan held onto regional practice. Diaspora communities preserved what the reform stripped out. So today, weirdly, a Western-trained practitioner who studies with a real lineage holder often ends up with more access to classical material than a mainland Chinese MD whose training was integrative from the start.

What got cut wasn't just a few rituals, there were actual layers to this thing. The biggest one I'd say is infrastructure. Classical CM assumes the practitioner is doing their own internal work, qigong, meditation, ritual hygiene, dietary discipline, all that stuff, because the work runs through your body and some days it's a lot depending on what the client is going through that day. A car won't run without gas, right? Without cultivation, the work just doesn't really run properly. The modern curriculum makes cultivation kind of optional, and most students treat it as a side hobby they'll get around to eventually, and then they wonder why their treatments aren't really landing.

Then there's the narrative layer of the medicine. Bai Hu Tang, you know, that's not just White Tiger Decoction as a brand name, that's a direct ritual reference to the mythical White Tiger of the West. Xiaoyao San is the Free and Easy Wanderer from Zhuangzi. Yin Ling Quan, Yin Mound Spring, that's literally where you put yin pathogenic factors to the grave. The names tell stories, every single one of them. Each channel has its own story too. The Lung meridian, for example, that's a priest performing the fengshan rites (封禪 fēng shàn) at the sacred mountain. The points along it reference that journey, each one carrying function and metaphor and mythology all together at once. Lung 1 is Zhong Fu, the Central Treasury where the priest gathers offerings before the ascent. Lung 2 is Yun Men, the Cloud Door. And so on through the channel.

And then there's the strategic piece. Acupuncture is more like the military side of things, tactics, immediate engagement, point-by-point work in the moment. Herbal medicine is more like the government, grand strategy, longer horizons, playing the long game. Formulas are actually organized by jun-chen-zuo-shi (君臣佐使) which translates to sovereign, minister, assistant, courier, so the formulas are essentially little governments of individuals working together. Guanzi said it best, a one-year plan, plant grains, a ten-year plan, plant trees, a lifetime plan, cultivate people. You can see where this is going.

Lineage and apprenticeship are basically cheat codes for this stuff, but they don't play the game for you, you still have to put in the hours yourself.

Every pre-modern medicine system had these kinds of ritual layers, by the way. European doctors used to do astrology as part of their practice. Persian medicine read star charts. The difference here is what the layers were actually doing. In classical CM, the cultivation infrastructure wasn't decorative, it was practitioner-attunement that made the needles and herbs land harder, do their work better. The reform cut the infrastructure but kept the needling, so the thing that actually made the medicine work got cut, but the part that looks like medicine stayed in place. Two different practitioners can use the exact same point selection and get totally different results, and the cultivation is usually the difference there.

What you’re holding when you write up a diagnosis is a report card on someone’s life, and you have to read it that way. Take shoulder pain for example, that’s almost always a relationship problem of some kind, the weight of someone or something you’re carrying. Menstrual issues, you’re typically looking at self-esteem about not being properly nourished. Lower back pain, that’s usually the world getting too heavy for the person.

In this view of things, the body is the table and the table is the universe, if that makes sense. The diagnostic language we’re using IS the magickal language. They’re not two different things.

Even the boring parts of the curriculum are magick. Wax on, wax off, Karate Kid had it right, your basics are your basics. Yin-yang from module one is its own divinatory and treatment system actually, Balance Method Acupuncture is built right on it. Five phases, your generation cycle is essentially a ritual to build something forward, the control cycle is essentially a ritual to restrain or cut. Seven emotions is a stat check on yourself, your masks, your internal selves, all that. They’re embodied, by the way, and if you don’t watch them they end up running things from the shadows whether you noticed it or not.

Board exam calls all of this theory. But it’s been working lenses of practical magic for two thousand years in the lineages I’ve worked with. It gets called theoretical because the school structure can’t really take you through the immersion that would make it not theoretical. In qigong training, you experience this stuff directly, and at that point theory just stops being theory. You are the medicine.

So Mao said publicly he didn’t believe in Chinese medicine, the memoirs back this up. The folk record though, that tells you he kept Daoist priest bodyguards with him on campaign and used qimen dunjia (奇門遁甲), the war-magic calculation system. Officially there was no superstition allowed in the new system, but unofficially basically every move he made was inside that framework anyway.

And the same split runs right through the textbooks today, surfaces in the moments that actually matter. Those COVID hospitals that got built in Wuhan in early 2020, they were named Huoshenshan 火神山 and Leishenshan 雷神山, Fire God Mountain and Thunder God Mountain. These are Five Elements names, Daoist deity names. They literally named the hospitals after the fire god and the thunder god to clear what TCM was reading as a yin pathogen. State medicine, formally materialist, naming buildings the old way the moment the stakes got real.

So when we get to the chapters on bazi diagnostics and ritual intervention and channel narratives, don’t file any of that stuff under the magick department for me. The procession of figures each meridian carries, the acupoints describing their journey, all of it. File the whole thing under medicine.

Same world either way, just two eyes looking at you, doesn’t really matter which one you focus on, you’re still looking at the same face anyway.

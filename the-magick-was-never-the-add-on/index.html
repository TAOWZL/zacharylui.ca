The Magick Was Never the Add-On

Sun Simiao wrote the incantation into the same book as the prescription. The Yellow Emperor's own classic says the ancients healed by the spell-cure alone, and the Yuan state examined charms and spells beside acupuncture. The magick was never a component of this medicine, because it was never a separate thing.

Sun Simiao (Sūn Sīmiǎo) is the closest thing this medicine has to a patron saint. King of Medicine (藥王 yàowáng). Deified after he died. Still on the syllabus thirteen centuries on, and every student meets his name in first year whether they clock it or not.

So open the formulary that made him canonical. A herb prescription dozens of ingredients deep, with the preparation and the dosing and the pulse behind it. And then, same book, same hand, the incantation you say over the person who drinks it. It sits in the running text, in the same voice as the doses, with no wall around it. It just runs on.

The usual explanation is tolerance. Broad-minded old doctor, kept an open mind, wrote down whatever he came across. That reading is wrong, and it's wrong in a way that's worth a chapter to you, because it quietly assumes there was a wall available and he chose not to build it. There was nothing standing on either side of it to keep apart. And if you trained in this medicine any time in the last fifty years, you got handed that wall as a fact about the tradition instead of a fact about the syllabus you were reading it in.

The thesis, said flat

Chapter one laid out the working parts of magic and asked how many of them are sitting in this medicine. Divination, sympathetic correspondence, energy work, and spirits called out and told what to do. So we came back with four out of five. So this chapter is the receipts for the fourth one, and the fourth one is the piece everybody assumes was never in the building.

Let me head off the friendly misreading first. Am I telling you medicine and magick were one undivided primal blur? I'm not. That's the romance version. They had job titles, specialties, regional traditions. Somebody who chanted for a living and somebody who dosed for a living knew perfectly well which one they were, right?

What they didn't have was two mechanisms. There was one account of how anything affects anything, and that account is resonance between things of the same kind. The drug, the needle and the spell were three ways of working that one account. Right. So when I say the magick was never the add-on, I'm making a claim about parts. And you cannot bolt on a thing that was never a separate part. So the next time you hear somebody say they've added a spiritual layer to their acupuncture, ask what they think was running underneath it before they added anything, because usually you're looking at the same engine with a new label on the front.

Now Strickmann, because this one costs me something. Michel Strickmann is the reason I can use the word at all in print. He wrote the definitive study of this material, it went out from Stanford under the title Chinese Magical Medicine, and he's the one who worked out what Sun Simiao's spell manual actually is. But look at what he was studying. Talismans, exorcism, demonology, the whole ritual corpus. Nobody disputes calling any of that magical. And it was never the contested ground.

The contested ground is the boring part, and you already know what the boring part is if you've sat the exam. Pattern diagnosis and point selection. So if I tell you the boring part runs on the same engine, that's a bigger claim than Strickmann made, and citing him doesn't buy it for me. He gets me the word. I have to earn the extension myself. So let's get down to the ground, because that's where the oldest evidence physically is.

Start in the ground

In 1973, diggers opened a tomb at Mawangdui sealed in 168 BCE and pulled out the earliest medical manuscripts we've recovered. Open the main recipe manual. A herbal remedy, and a spell to say over it. Same ailment, same page, same register. It should surprise you. Dozens of the recipes in there are flat-out incantation and ritual, shelved right in among the drugs. And the era could tell spell-folk from drug-folk when it wanted to. So this isn't ignorance, right? Nobody bothered to enforce it on the page.

Donald Harper translated these manuscripts, and he defends "magico-religious" as the right description of the early picture. Those two words don't name opposite categories in early China. They're one field. So if you've been told this medicine started clean and the ritual got smuggled in later, you have to account for the oldest thing we own coming out of the ground with the spell and the drug already sharing a page.

These also aren't vague blessings tucked into a margin. Some of the recipes tell you to address the ailment directly, by name, like a thing that can hear you. One of the scorpion-sting recipes, in Harper's translation, opens "Spit on it and spout," and then it hands you the words. "Father dwells in Shu. Mother is the Wind Bird who punishes. Do not dare flee up or down. The Wind Bird bores your heart." That's in the same flat instructional voice as the drug doses beside it. So if you're picturing a nervous villager muttering something over a wound, put that picture down. What you're looking at is a technical manual. That's a procedure.

Now look at the structure, because the structure is the point. It names the affliction. It addresses it as an agent. It tells it what's coming. In that order. The saying is the act itself, and Tambiah spent a career on exactly that.

I want to fence that immediately, because there's a lazy version of this argument going around. Does it mean acupoint names are magic words? It does not. Waiguan is Outer Pass because of where it sits and what it does. You've got three different jobs here. In an incantation, the name is aimed at something and the saying is the working. In the nomenclature, the name is a label doing a label's job. And a pattern name is a third thing again. It points. Liver qi stagnation tells you the Liver's job of moving qi smoothly isn't getting done, which is accurate and meaningful and useless on its own. And that's most of what's wrong with a curriculum. You get the word in second year, you get the case in third, and if you're lucky somebody puts your hand on a wrist at some stage and says feel that, that's the thing the word was for.

Only the first of those three is an operation. And I'll name it in the vocabulary of my other trade. Evocation is calling something up outside yourself. In front of you, where you can address it and give it orders. Invocation is calling something into yourself. Taking it on. Letting it ride you. My teacher Andrieh Vitimus lays the distinction out in Hands-On Chaos Magic. Is that fussy pedantry? I don't think it is. Different jobs, different risks, different protective measures. People get hurt confusing them.

So which one is the scorpion recipe? It's evocation, and the affliction is out there. That's the shape of nearly everything in this chapter. The inward-facing operation is in chapter four, where it's the state of the operator himself. And of everything spirit-facing in this chapter, that's the only piece still on the syllabus.

The classic tells its own origin story, and it isn't the one you were taught

This is the one that got me. It's in the Su Wen. And it isn't tucked in some Daoist grimoire or a folk manual. It's the Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic, the thing every student buys in first year and then mostly doesn't read.

Chapter 13 is called the Discourse on Moving the Essence and Changing the Qi (移精變氣論). That's how Unschuld renders it. The Yellow Emperor opens it by asking a question about history.

I've heard that when the ancients treated illness, they only moved the essence and changed the qi. The spell-cure (祝由), and that was the end of it. Today we treat illness with toxic drugs for the interior and needle and stone for the exterior, and sometimes the patient recovers and sometimes he doesn't. Why is that?

Lin Fu-shih has a whole article on what 祝由 means in this passage, and he finds the field split three ways. Incantation (咒說). Invocatory prayer (祝禱). Ritual announcement (祝告). "Spell-cure" is a fair rendering of the first, and it's the one I use. All three are speech acts and two of the three are unambiguously ritual. If you go and read Lin's article yourself and come back convinced that ritual announcement is the better reading, I'd say you've still got a ritual sitting at the head of the canon, and you should tell me I overcooked the word.

Qi Bo's answer is the part that keeps me honest, so you get it in full instead of the flattering half. He says the ancients lived among birds and beasts. They moved to keep off the cold and sat in the shade to keep off the heat. They had no clinging desires inside them and no ambition for office outside. A placid age. That's his picture. In that world, he says, evil (邪) couldn't penetrate deeply, and that's why drugs couldn't treat the interior and needle and stone couldn't treat the exterior. They weren't needed. You could shift the essence and work the spell, and that was the whole of it. Is the present age like that? He says it isn't. Worry inside, labour outside, thieving winds arriving constantly, and illness reaching all the way to the five depots and the marrow. And so, he says, the spell-cure alone can no longer end it.

Now take the hostile reading. That's a golden-age story. Chinese texts are full of them, and the Su Wen opens with one. Qi Bo's reasoning is also medical instead of devotional. Shallow disease yields to ritual, deep disease doesn't. If anything it's an argument for needling, on the grounds that the world got worse and we needed stronger tools.

Fine. Take all of that. What's left is still enormous. The founding text of this medicine gets asked where the art came from. It answers the spell-cure. It doesn't say there was also some folk ritual knocking around at the time. It says the art itself. And the thing the ancients were doing has a name, moving the essence and changing the qi (移精變氣), and it isn't an operation on tissue. It's an operation on a person's essence-spirit and qi. And you can check this yourself in about twenty minutes, because Unschuld's translation sits in most school libraries and chapter 13 is short, and I'd rather you read it than take my summary of it. Go and look.

I was never taught that chapter. Do I think somebody hid it? I don't know, and I doubt it. There's a lot of Su Wen and a two-year program has to leave things out. But it's chapter 13 of 81, and I'd been in this medicine for years before I read it properly.

The word only points. Don't lean on it.

Now the word for the thing I do, because people keep reading too much into it. Me included, for a while.

Pull apart the standard character, 醫 (yī). A quiver of arrows and a hand gripping a weapon on top, and 酉 (yǒu), a wine vessel, at the base. A cutter and a druggist. Nice story, isn't it? Except the stricter philology reads that whole top half as the word's sound instead of a picture of its work, so the pretty version is on thin ice before we start. And there's no shaman in it.

But there is an old attested way of writing the same word, the variant 毉. Same arrows and hand on top. And at the base, where the wine jar sat, you get 巫, the shaman. Does it prove the shaman came first? It doesn't. The best etymology going is Schuessler's, and it traces the word back to "potion." Back to the wine jar. You'll see the shaman version of this graph on a lot of clinic websites and in a lot of first-year lectures, mine included at one point, and it makes a lovely slide, and it will not hold your weight if a philologist leans on it.

Here's what does carry weight. 巫 (wū), the spirit-medium, turns up in the oracle bones, older in the record than the book-trained physician. And what did a wū do? There's no tidy English word you can hand somebody, because he did all of it at once. Medium, healer, exorcist, reader of signs.

Then the two characters get written together. 巫醫, wū yī. Shaman and doctor in one phrase, and that's what people reach for when they want to say the two roles were one job. The locus classicus is the Analects at 13.22. And you should have the objection I'd want handed to me if it cut the other way. A lot of sinologists read that line as a coordinate pair. Shamans and doctors, two kinds of people listed together, instead of a welded job title. If they're right, my punchiest version of this goes away, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. So what survives? The two words sat comfortably in one phrase, and the phrase went on being used for people who did both. Lo and Cullen document that out of the Dunhuang material. That's the part I need.

"Acupuncturist" and "herbalist" are translation casualties

Now the English words, because they've been quietly doing something to you. "Acupuncturist." "Herbalist." Two trades, two people, or so the words imply. But the Chinese for the first is 針灸 (zhēn jiǔ), and that's two characters. 針 is the needle. And 灸 is the one everybody gets wrong. Me included, until somebody corrected me in public. We'll get to him.

灸 is written 久 above 火. 火 is the fire and it carries the meaning. 久 sits on top and gives the sound. So resist the pretty reading where the two halves add up to "long fire." That gloss is everywhere. It sounds like insight, right? And it isn't what the graph is doing.

So what's missing from the character? Mugwort is missing, and 艾 ài is a completely different character. It appears nowhere in the word, and it was sitting right there available to anybody who wanted to put it in. And nobody put it in. The word says fire applied to the body, and it doesn't say what you burn. Which means the thing you were taught to call moxibustion is a category with an open slot in it, and mugwort is what eventually got put in the slot and then got mistaken for the slot itself.

So the scholars working the early material don't render it "moxibustion." They render it cauterization. Harper does it throughout, on the strength of what the manuscripts themselves describe.

And here I have to take away a piece of evidence I used to like. Those two vessel texts, the oldest we own, get cited everywhere as the Cauterization Canons of the Eleven Vessels (足臂十一脈灸經 and 陰陽十一脈灸經). I've quoted the titles that way myself. But the Han didn't choose them. The silk came out of the tomb untitled, and the names were assigned by the modern editorial group that worked the Mawangdui find after the 1973 excavation. So they're a 1970s shelving decision. Twentieth century. And they tell you nothing about what a Han physician called his own book. If a textbook pulled that move I'd have just spent a page telling you not to trust it, right? So the titles come out. Does the argument lose anything? I don't think it does, and the point stands up better resting on the manuscripts anyway.

The category also stayed wider than mugwort. Sky cauterization (天灸 tiān jiǔ) binds hot-natured herbs onto the point. There's no flame in it anywhere. Still 灸.

So the English is a two-way loss instead of one. "Acupuncture" keeps the needle and drops the fire, and not for lack of a word, because "moxibustion" is right there in the dictionary. But "moxibustion" is its own casualty running the other way. English dropped the fire, went back for it, and came home holding one plant. And you've been using that English word your whole career without anybody telling you it was a translation decision, made by people who had to pick one word and picked the narrowest one on the table. I didn't get a flag on it either.

I had all of this wrong in an earlier version.

A man called Johan Hausen put it in the comments. Sixteen hours after I posted. He's like, to be fully precise, even burning of mugwort is incorrect, 灸 is best translated as cauterization, there are types that don't resort to mugwort at all.

I read it, and then I read it again. That's the part nobody warns you about with publishing. You put a thing up, you're pleased with it, it's been out for less than a day, and somebody who knows the material better than you turns up in the box underneath it in front of everyone. I sat with that for a minute. Nobody enjoys that minute. Then I went, yeah. Okay, he's right. There was nothing in there to argue with, and I've watched plenty of people in this field argue anyway. So I hearted the comment. And then I went and looked at the graph, because looking at the graph is the job. The Shuowen Jiezi (說文解字) gives 灸 as 灼也，从火久聲. To burn. From fire, with 久 supplying the sound.

Which is the whole method in one exchange, honestly. He was right. The textbook I'd taken my version from was wrong. And the graph had a further detail in it that I'd have got wrong in the other direction if I hadn't gone and looked. So that's three corrections out of one comment, and I only made one of them myself.

He also runs Purple Cloud Press, and Purple Cloud published the Saam monograph I'd spent chapter five saying didn't exist in English. It exists. So that's twice he had me. It's in chapter five's reading list now, under his imprint. That's what checking somebody's work looks like when it goes well, and I'd rather you saw one that went well.

"Herbalist" is thinner still. The Chinese category, materia medica (本草 běn cǎo), was never just plants. Minerals, animal parts, and substances prepared closer to alchemy than to gardening. And who handled all of it? The same person who held the needle. He was the doctor (醫). One job.

So let me state the loss precisely. The old word 醫 covered a physician who needled and burned and dosed and read pulses and, when it came to it, dealt with the spirits and the dead. The English titles keep the first several jobs and drop the last one. The ancestors were in the old word. They fell out in translation.

It was on the exam

And the splitting wasn't a Western sin done to a Chinese whole, either. The Chinese ran the licensing themselves. And for a long stretch it says the opposite of what you'd expect.

Under the Yuan, the Imperial Academy of Medicine examined in thirteen subjects. The roster below is the standard one, and it comes to me through the Great Dictionary of Chinese Medicine (中醫大辭典) and the reference works downstream of it instead of a dated primary edict I can put in front of you. Internal medicine, miscellaneous medicine, pediatrics, wind disorders, obstetrics. Then eye medicine, mouth and teeth, throat, bone-setting, wounds and swellings. Then, eleventh, needle and fire (針灸科).

Twelfth, the spell-cure (祝由科). Philip Cho, who wrote the study of it, calls it exorcism.

Thirteenth, interdiction, or spell-binding (禁科).

So ritual healing wasn't whispered about at the back door, right? The state examined it. The state issued the licence for it, in the two seats immediately after acupuncture. Right. And Cho reads the spell-cure (祝由) as a synthetic and liminal practice that resists being sorted into elite or popular at all, which is pretty much the opposite of a survival from somewhere backward.

Then the part that cuts against me. The spell-cure (祝由) sits at twelve of thirteen, below acupuncture. And it doesn't stay on the roster. In 1571 the Ming academy struck it off along with the massage department, and the interdiction department at thirteen had already gone two centuries earlier at the founding of the dynasty. So who was contesting this? Chinese physicians, from inside the profession, roughly four hundred years before Yu Yunxiu ever picked up a pen. Lin Fu-shih's correction is worth more than his date. Abolition had little to do with any quarrel with orthodox medicine. What 1571 took was the licence. The practice carried on without it.

Was my claim ever that everybody agreed? It wasn't. My claim is about what kind of system this is. And a system doesn't stop running on resonance because a bureau reorganized its departments. What it stops doing is admitting it in public, on paper, with a stamp on it. A thing losing its department is not the same as a thing losing its function. Watch which one you're being sold.

Sun Simiao didn't bother coding any of it

Back to the King of Medicine, because he's the centre of this and not the margin. Tang dynasty, died 682. He wrote the two formularies the prescription tradition rests on, the Bèijí Qiānjīn Yàofāng (備急千金要方) and its sequel the Qiānjīn Yìfāng (千金翼方). Thousands of formulas, pulse diagnosis, acupuncture, moxa.

The sequel also carries what scholars call his Book of Spellbinding (禁經 jìnjīng). Talismans, incantations, exorcisms, and acupuncture points listed specifically for driving out demons. Strickmann studied this material more closely than anyone, and he thinks Sun must have trained as a Daoist priest, because only an initiate would have known the rites, and that's a circumstantial case and not a signed confession.

And was it one man's quirk about where to file things? I don't think you can read it that way. Sun opens his whole work not with technique but with the doctor's character. A still heart, real compassion, and the same care given to the rich man, the poor man, and the enemy. So the instrument comes before the formula. Where does your own training put that conversation, if it puts it anywhere? That's the energy work again, if you're keeping count. And the King of Medicine put it on page one.

But the classics attacked the shamans

Right. They did. And it's the strongest objection to everything above, so it gets a straight answer.

By the time the Yellow Emperor's classic was compiled, elite medicine was drawing a line, and it was drawing it against the wu. One Su Wen passage runs, in effect, that with someone bound up with ghosts and spirits you cannot even begin to talk about real medicine. Later on it gets worse and more organized. The Song state ran campaigns against wu (巫) healing in the south, and TJ Hinrichs has documented that in detail. So a skeptic reads all that and says, well, there's your answer. The serious tradition cut the spirit-work loose a long time ago, and your Yuan exam and your Sun Simiao are the exceptions that prove the rule.

So why don't I think it lands? It comes down to what the contempt is made of. Robert Hymes wrote the study on Song and Yuan physicians and called it "Not Quite Gentlemen?" Educated doctors working extremely hard to be counted as literati, inside a status order with no obvious slot for them. And when you're a marginal professional clawing for respectability, who do you most need to not be mistaken for? The unlettered guy down the road doing the same job for less money with a drum. That's a status argument, right? Class and credential, right? Cosmology has very little to do with it.

And you can check whether it was ever an argument about mechanism, because there's one question that settles it. When the scholar-physicians won, what did they throw out?

They kept resonance. They kept the five phases. They kept the stems and branches, the elected hour, the pattern logic, the requirement that the operator govern his own spirit before he treats. All of it. They dropped a class of person. And you don't get to say "not magic" while you're operating the whole apparatus. You're saying "not those people." And that distinction is worth holding onto, because you will meet it again in your own training, dressed up as rigour, every time somebody tells you a technique is unscientific while writing you a prescription that runs on the five phases.

Now the counter to my counter, because Hinrichs wouldn't sign what I just wrote without amendment. Her reading is that the Song campaign was a genuine transformation of medical governance, and that the literati really did believe textual, drug-based, correlative medicine was better medicine. Epistemic arguments, not only social ones. So the fair version is that class and credential were doing at least as much work as epistemology. And I'll take that version. It's enough for what I need. And if you want to argue with me about it, argue with Hinrichs first, because she has done the archival work and I have read the archival work, and those are two very different things. You're getting the second one from me.

And on the historiography generally, Vivienne Lo has the line I'd put on the wall. The split between "religious" and "secular," "scholarly" and "folk" healing is a line later historians drew, and the sources don't support it. What the excavated manuscripts show, in her words, is the intricately linked worlds of diviner and physician and their shared iatromantic culture. Shared. From somebody with better credentials than mine.

Why this matters away from the library

Fine, you might say. The exam is a good fact, Sun is a party trick, and it's all centuries dead. Does any of it change anything once a living person is in the room with you? So let's take it.

Watch one appointment run two ways. A man comes in for insomnia. On a day I'm working the intake form and nothing else, I take the complaint at its word. Points for sleep. The sleep-hygiene conversation. Book the follow-up, see you in a week. Done. On a day I'm reading the rest of the room, something else happens. He flinches when his brother comes up. And then, while he's getting on the table, not as a symptom, just as conversation, he mentions that he's been sleeping downstairs since the funeral. A minute later he says something about how quiet the place is now. And nothing on the intake form has a line for any of that. It says insomnia.

The soft version of the story is that the second version of me is just nicer. It isn't about nicer. It's a bigger correspondence table. Grief has a category in this system. An organ, a phase, a season, a sound, a direction. So when the man says the place is too quiet, I'm not collecting a poignant detail because I'm a sensitive listener. It's an entry in the table, in the same column as the lung and the autumn and the weeping. And the sleep is in that column too. So the question I'm asking myself isn't whether the man is sad, it's which column he's sitting in and what the rest of that column predicts I'll find when I take his pulse.

That isn't my observation, by the way, and I'd rather cite it than claim it. Judith Farquhar sat in Chinese clinics through the 1980s, watching senior doctors work and listening to how they talked their way to a decision, and she wrote Knowing Practice out of what she actually saw them do. What she documents is that the correspondence language isn't ornament laid over a decision made some other way. It's the machinery the decision comes out of. An ethnographer watching real clinicians is better evidence than anything I can tell you about my own Tuesday afternoon.

So the first version of the appointment didn't fail at empathy. It was working from a smaller chart. Does the extra column help anybody? That's not a question this book can answer.

Same fence as chapter one. I'm telling you what kind of reasoning this is. I'm not telling you it cured his sleep. You can work the intake form and nothing else your whole career and do honest, regulated work. And when what surfaces in the room is grief that needs its own professional, that referral gets made, and it gets made early. But you can't work with what you never let yourself look at. And you can't look at what your chart has no column for. So the question was never whether you believe any of this, because belief wasn't the entry requirement. They handed you a table and told you which column to read down. That's the whole ask.

Sun Simiao put the spell and the decoction in the same book because as far as he was concerned it was one book. So nobody has to add the magick back into this medicine. The paperwork changed. The table didn't. And whatever you end up doing with that, whether you're in the medicine or just reading about it, you're now holding the same table Sun Simiao was holding when he wrote the incantation in under the dose. And I'd rather practise knowing that than practise not knowing it.

Want to dig into the actual record

Same deal as last chapter. This is where the claims come from, so you can check my work.

The anchor. Michel Strickmann, Chinese Magical Medicine, edited by Bernard Faure (Stanford University Press, 2002). The book this whole series is arguing alongside, the definitive treatment of Sun Simiao's Book of Spellbinding, the 禁經, in the closing chapters of the Qianjin Yifang, and the source of the circumstantial case that Sun trained as a Daoist priest. Mawangdui, and the word 灸. Donald Harper, Early Chinese Medical Literature (Kegan Paul International, Sir Henry Wellcome Asian Series, 1998). Harper translates the manuscripts from the tomb sealed in 168 BCE, documents the exorcistic-incantation recipes interspersed among the drug recipes in the Recipes for Fifty-Two Ailments, and defends "magico-religious" as a category on the grounds that the two terms don't define opposite categories in the early Chinese conception. He renders 灸 as cauterization rather than moxibustion throughout, and that choice, plus the character's use in the manuscript bodies, is the whole basis for what I say about the word. The scorpion-sting incantation is Harper's translation. Correcting my own earlier version: the two vessel texts from the same tomb are cited everywhere as 足臂十一脈灸經 and 陰陽十一脈灸經, but those titles are modern, assigned by the editorial group that prepared the Mawangdui materials after the 1973 excavation. The silk came out of the ground untitled. They are not evidence of Han usage and I no longer use them as such. The counter-case, that mugwort was probably already the agent at this stage, is made in the unearthed-literature scholarship on early moxibustion. My point is about the character, not the smoke.

What 祝由 means in Su Wen 13. 林富士,〈「祝由」釋義：以《黃帝內經‧素問》為核心文本的討論〉,《中央研究院歷史語言研究所集刊》83.4 (2012), pp. 671-738, is where the three readings come from and where you should go before you take my rendering. Lin is also the source for the 1571 abolition date and for the argument that abolition had little to do with any quarrel with orthodox medicine. Chapter one has why most histories give a different date.

Su Wen 13. 移精變氣論, in Paul U. Unschuld and Hermann Tessenow's Huang Di Nei Jing Su Wen: An Annotated Translation (University of California Press, 2011), chapter 13, "Discourse on Moving the Essence and Changing the Qi." Read Qi Bo's answer in full, not just the Emperor's question.

The word. Axel Schuessler's ABC Etymological Dictionary of Old Chinese (University of Hawai'i Press) gives the root sense of 醫 as "potion," then "physician," and traces 巫 back to the oracle bones. I lean on it precisely to stay out of the folk-etymology trap. The variant graph 毉 is in the standard character dictionaries, and that's as far as I'll take it, because the secondary discussions that get quoted around are harder to run down than they look. Paul Unschuld's Medicine in China: A History of Ideas (University of California Press) treats the wu healers and the rise of the Confucian scholar-physician.

巫醫, and the argument about it. The locus classicus is Analects 13.22, and the reading is genuinely contested. Many translators take 巫醫 there as a coordinate pair, "shamans and doctors," rather than a single compound job title, which is why I flag it in the body. For the pairing as a real working category in the medieval material, Vivienne Lo and Christopher Cullen, Medieval Chinese Medicine: The Dunhuang Medical Manuscripts (RoutledgeCurzon, 2005), which also records the medieval Daoist and Buddhist attacks on those figures.

The Yuan exam. 元史·百官志, the Yuan history's treatise on offices, carries the institutional record of the 太醫院, the Imperial Academy of Medicine. The thirteen-subject roster itself circulates through later compilations and reference works, notably 中醫大辭典 and everything downstream of it, rather than through a dated primary edict. One disambiguation, because you'll trip on it the moment you search. The popular ritual manual titled 祝由十三科 has nothing to do with the Yuan list, its thirteen being its own internal divisions, and Chinese web sources smear the two together constantly. Reiko Shinno's The Politics of Chinese Medicine Under Mongol Rule (Routledge, Needham Research Institute Series) is the study of the politics around the academy. For 祝由 as elite rather than marginal medicine, Philip S. Cho, "Healing and Ritual Imagination in Chinese Medicine: The Multiple Interpretations of Zhuyou," East Asian Science, Technology, and Medicine 38 (2013), p. 71. His doctoral work, Ritual and the Occult in Chinese Medicine and Religious Healing: The Development of Zhuyou Exorcism (University of Pennsylvania), argues the exorcism framing at length, which is why I use that word.

Correspondence as the thing that generates the clinical decision. Judith Farquhar, Knowing Practice: The Clinical Encounter of Chinese Medicine (Westview Press, 1994). Ethnography of Chinese clinics, and the standard treatment of what this chapter's last section is about, which is pattern reasoning producing the decision instead of decorating it. If you only half believe me when I say the correspondence table is the engine and not the wallpaper, read Farquhar instead of me.

On evocation versus invocation. Andrieh Vitimus, Hands-On Chaos Magic: Reality Manipulation through the Ovayki Current (Llewellyn, 2009). He's my teacher, so read that disclosure into it, but the distinction is standard across the Western magical literature and he states it cleanly.

The status argument. Robert P. Hymes, "Not Quite Gentlemen? Doctors in Sung and Yuan," Chinese Science 8 (1987), pp. 9-76, is the keystone on physicians' status anxiety. TJ Hinrichs is the specialist on the Song state's campaigns against 巫 healing in the south. The book is Shamans, Witchcraft, and Quarantine: The Medical Transformation of Governance and Southern Customs in Mid-Imperial China (Harvard East Asian Monographs). The shorter way in is "Governance Through Medical Texts and the Role of Print," in Lucille Chia, ed., Knowledge and Text Production in an Age of Print: China, 900-1400 (Brill, 2011), pp. 217-238. Her reading is more substantive than mine, so read her against me. Vivienne Lo, "But is it [History of] Medicine? Twenty Years in the History of the Healing Arts of China," Social History of Medicine 22:2 (2009), pp. 283-303, is where the "shared iatromantic culture" line comes from.

The big picture. Geoffrey Lloyd and Nathan Sivin, The Way and the Word (Yale University Press, 2002), on how medicine in early China took shape among the divinatory and cosmological arts. One caution I owe you. The Han imperial bibliography filed medicine (方技) and the divinatory arts (數術) in separate divisions. Adjacent, both low-prestige, bibliographically distinct. So "medicine and divination were the same thing" is too strong. "They lived in the same technical-arts world and the practitioners overlapped" is what the record supports and what I'm claiming.

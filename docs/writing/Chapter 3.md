# Chinese Medicine Lost Its Licence in 1571

There was a ritual therapy department inside the Chinese state medical bureaucracy for about a thousand years. It was struck off the roster in 1571. Here are the dates on all of it, and what a licence ending does and doesn't mean.

## Dates on the eight

### The ritual department, and the day it closed

I'll start with the ritual department. It sat inside the state medical bureaucracy for about a thousand years, and it kept getting renamed as the dynasties turned over. Three names, one department. Lin Fu-shih spent a career on this, and he traces it as one line, not three similar things. It ran for longer than your profession has had a college. The names are in the reading list for you.

It ended in 1571. Struck off the roster, alongside the massage department. The date itself is standard, it's in the ordinary reference books, so honestly, don't take it as mine or as Lin's. What I take from Lin is the continuity. One line, all the way down. Plus his finding that most of the medical histories you'd actually read skip the abolition or get the date wrong. Want the actual statute? The Collected Statutes of the Great Ming.

### The divination side, and the exam paper that proves it

The divination side has a date too, and it isn't the one I'd have picked. The point-by-the-clock methods, the ones that pick your point off the hour and the day, off the calendar, basically, rather than off the patient in front of you, were never on a state curriculum. They circulated as texts, and that's all. What got examined is the five periods and six qi, the doctrine that reads the year off the calendar. It got into the Su Wen in the eighth century and then, basically, became a tested subject in the Song medical bureau. And the receipt for that is a book. A book of model exam papers from the bureau. He Daren put it together inside the bureau, and his preface is dated 1212. Later that year he asks to get the papers printed, so candidates out in the provinces know the standard. Pulse, formulas, case questions, and periods and qi, right there on the paper. And how many did one man face? Under the bureau's own rules, two questions on periods and qi, every stream, needlers included. Two questions on a calendrical forecast, right? Picture your own licensing exam with that section still in it.

Now here's the part I'd want from somebody else, so I'll give it to you. What we read is a reconstruction, and not He Daren's book as he issued it. Qing editors pieced it back together in 1781 out of an imperial encyclopedia, eighty-seven answers from more than one sitting. Not one man's paper. Which is why I gave you the bureau's rule for the count instead.

So that's one of the eight. Where were the rest of them sitting? Two of them were in that line of departments I just closed, and they sat in it together, the calling of spirits and the writing of objects that bind. You want the state's own word for it? By 1212 the Song bureau's own head was counting its departments, thirteen of them, general medicine first and written interdiction last, and written interdiction is short for talisman-writing and spell-interdiction. They put the writing in the title, right? Whether the Tang licence stretched that far is a real argument, and it's in the reading list.

And the last five never needed a department at all. Three are in the canon from early and they never leave. The correspondence work, as the five-phase mapping of the transport points and the flavours going to the organs. The energy work, as governing the spirit (治神), and that's Su Wen 25, the line you met in chapter two. For needling to be real, first govern the spirit. And the energy work is older than the canon, on chapter two's jade cylinder, around 380 BCE. And the gnosis, as the state that same chapter tells you to be in before the needle goes in. All three are still in your training, right, the mapping as the transport points, the energy work as an elective, and the gnosis filed under concentration. The last two sit in books rather than in an institution. Sun Simiao put the self-work at the front of his formulary in the seventh century, ahead of the formulas, and that's the illumination one. And Zhang Congzheng put the expulsion logic in writing in the thirteenth, which is the banishing. So one examined, two licensed, five needing nobody's permission. Same five chapter two said you can't pull out of the clinical act. That's your eight, and you can audit every line of it.

### Why reading the year is divination

The tradition filed periods and qi under medicine. It taught it as medicine and examined it as medicine. Here's how I read it. What matters is what the operation does, whatever they filed it under. Reading a year off a stem-and-branch cycle to predict what will sicken people is the same operation, whatever shelf it was catalogued on. Run it through the test from chapter two. Can you walk the line from a stem-and-branch year back to a fever in a village? There's no line to walk, and that's what puts it on the list.

So what's the number you want? A thousand years for the department, Sui down to Ming. And if you'll only take hard paper on both ends, three hundred and fifty-nine. 1212 out to 1571, and 1212 is the date on He Daren's preface, so it's the day I can put my finger on. The bureau's rules put periods and qi on the exam a century earlier, you know, but those reach us as a summary of a curriculum. A dated preface is paper you can hold, so that's where I start. And that isn't the age of the medicine, so don't go filing it next to the seventy-five. What it is, is the span between a dated preface on the state's own exam papers for one of these, and the last hard receipt for another being licensed by it.

1571 didn't stop anybody. That's Lin again. Physicians were still defending incantation therapy into the Qing. Centuries of people in your job, arguing for the thing your program doesn't mention. Think of it as a language that loses its official status and keeps every one of its speakers. It carried on in folk transmission into the twentieth century, and down to my own teachers. So what did 1571 actually take away? The licence.

**The magick never left. It just lost its licence.**

After that the losses keep coming. The Qing struck the acupuncture department out of the Imperial Academy of Medicine, in the nineteenth century. I'd call that a bigger hit to the machinery than 1571 was, and you'll hear about it even less. The date's in the reading list. Yu Yunxiu's motion in 1929 isn't on this list, because it never became law. Then the 1950s, which didn't touch a licence at all. Why would they? There was nothing left to revoke by then, and the only thing anybody could still rewrite was the curriculum. So they rewrote the curriculum.

## What got cut was what the frame asked of you

Now maybe you're a practitioner reading this and going, wait. Plenty of these methods are still in mainland TCM. So what got cut exactly? Fair question. Two things going on here.

**First, the frame around the methods got swapped.** Classical CM held all of this inside a Chinese cosmological and ritual context, Daoist and Buddhist and Confucian and folk-religious strands woven in, depending on the lineage and the period. Post-reform TCM holds it inside a Marxist-materialist and biomedical-adjacent context. Same points, different universe described around them.

So the swap left the reasoning standing and changed what the reasoning was for. Inside that swap there are three layers. The vocabulary, which changed completely. The structure of the operation, which didn't. And what the operation asks of you, which is the one this section is about.

I lean on Nathan Sivin here, the historian of Chinese science. What I take from him is narrow. He translated and studied the 1972 *Revised Outline of Chinese Medicine*, a textbook written to teach this material to biomedically trained physicians, and what he showed is how much old correlative reasoning is still carrying the load underneath the materialist presentation. He used it to measure the change. I use it to measure what kept running. Read him and decide for yourself.

So say Liver qi stagnation, needle Taichong (Liver 3). What did you just run? A correspondence operation. And the structure runs whether or not you've got a name for it, which is exactly why it survived the swap. What it asks of you is a separate question, and that's the one the swap answered differently.

It's like a courthouse. The oath, the robes, the standing up and sitting down, the reading-in of a charge. Every one of those is a ritual form that still does real work in that room. And nobody in it would call the day's business a rite. Your clinic's the same. You're standing inside an older machine that never stopped running. Strip out every talisman, and you've still got a resonance engine sitting there, because the resonance engine *is* the medicine.

**Second, the public naming went underground.** The state doesn't officially endorse ritual or spirit or magick, not as part of clinical medicine. So classical methods that depend on those frames get reframed in materialist language, or they get quietly dropped. Some ritual practice survives in regulated forms, state-recognized qigong, registered Daoist temples, some Buddhist medical contexts. But inside the standardized medical curriculum, the magickal frame is not on your menu.

Chapter one said their textbooks went on to render 邪 (xié, evil) as "pathogenic factor." Here's that character, in 邪氣 (xiéqì, evil qi). That rendering sounds like it came out of a microbiology lab. It didn't. 邪 means crooked, deviant, heterodox, malign, and it's the same character sitting in the term for driving off evil spirits (驅邪). And take the graph apart before you let me lean on it, because it pays. The Shuowen, the standard character dictionary, files it under a place name in Shandong, so the moral sense rides on it as a loan. Your textbook's most technical-sounding word sits on a graph the dictionary reads as a postcode. Unschuld renders it "evil qi." Wiseman's dictionary makes it "evil." Sivin uses "heteropathic," to get the moral charge out of it. Three serious translators. Two positions. And the split runs straight down the middle of the problem. Unschuld and Wiseman both keep the moral charge. Sivin built a word to get rid of it. So what does that tell you? Probably that it isn't a word with a clean technical history. It's a word people keep having to do something about. Now, the medicine did build a graded physical model around evil qi as well, seasons and winds and depletion, paired against upright qi (正氣). So it isn't demons all the way down, and I'm not saying it is. What never got rebuilt is the moral sense underneath it. The word got a new coat of paint, you know, and the thing it names kept its old shape, which is why the translators can't agree on it.

The techniques are all still there, on paper, right? The reasoning survived the swap too, that's the Taichong point. What got cut was what the old frame required of the person running them. Somebody driving a spirit out and somebody releasing a pathogenic factor are running exactly the same structure, and they are not asking the same thing of themselves while they run it. The hand is identical. What's behind the hand isn't, and this medicine has always said the thing behind the hand is the part that decides what kind of act it is. I mean what it is, and not whether it works.

## So this is the claim

**Chinese medicine is a magickal system.**

That's stronger than the polite version people reach for when they're trying to be generous to the tradition, a medicine with some magic attached. It's stronger than the nostalgic one too, a medicine that used to have magic and lost it.

Six of the eight are present tense, and I mean that literally, so take them family by family.

Divination first. The medicine reads the year off a calendar, today, in every school that still teaches the great treatises.

Then sympathetic correspondence. It runs on it today, in a clinic near you, and even the herbal formula names give it away. White Tiger Decoction (白虎湯) is the textbook's heat-clearing formula, and the White Tiger, in the myth, is the one that culls. The beast of metal, it cuts, claws out in the mountains of the west, in autumn, the cutting season. Cheng Wuji said so back in the twelfth century, the White Tiger is the metal spirit of the west. So read the formula in layers. The herbs carry a state of their own, their spirit or their energy, and that state is separate from you. On top of that, the name calls in the White Tiger itself. The first layer is still taught, as each herb's nature and flavour, and it's taught off a chart. You learn it the way you'd learn a table, and nobody shows you how to sit with the herb and meet it. The second is spirit work, one of the two the curriculum cut, and the name is what's left of it.

Then the three that live in you, the practitioner. Energy work, because the medicine works with an energy it treats as real. Gnosis, because it still tells you what state to be in before the needle goes in. Illumination, because it still tells you to work on yourself first, in Sun Simiao's chapters on becoming a great physician, which sit in front of his formulas.

And banishing. It still expels, today, naming the thing and choosing its exit, and that reasoning is sitting right underneath every "clear" and "drain" you charted this week.

That's six in the medicine. Chapter two's five and a half is your clinic.

And let me be precise about the subject of that claim, because chapter six is going to make me pay for it otherwise. I'm talking about the reasoning. How does this medicine decide what's wrong with you, and what to do about it? That's the thing I'm naming.

**On the character 邪.** The 說文解字 Shuowen, around 100 CE, gives 邪 as 琅邪郡 Langya commandery, a place in Shandong, 从邑牙聲, the city radical with 牙 for the sound. Now the translators. Go compare them yourself. Unschuld's translations render it "evil qi." Nigel Wiseman and Feng Ye's *A Practical Dictionary of Chinese Medicine* (Paradigm, 1998) standardizes it as "evil." And Sivin's *Traditional Medicine in Contemporary China* (University of Michigan Center for Chinese Studies, 1987), which uses "heteropathic" to avoid both. The disagreement, that's the evidence. And one more rendering, because it's the load-bearing one and it isn't in the list above. "Pathogenic factor" has a date and an address. It gets fixed for English readers by Cheng Xinnong's *Chinese Acupuncture and Moxibustion* (Beijing: Foreign Languages Press, 1987), the state-sanctioned teaching text, which uses it throughout. And the World Health Organization's *International Standard Terminologies on Traditional Medicine in the Western Pacific Region* (2007) doesn't endorse it as the headword: at entry 1.6.3 it standardizes 邪, 邪氣 and 病邪 as "pathogen," with "pathogenic factor" and "pathogenic qi" listed only as alternates. So that's four renderings and a standards body, split on whether to keep the moral charge. And Sivin is where the materialist-reframing argument comes from, in the narrow form I use it: his study of the 1972 *Revised Outline* shows how much correlative reasoning is still carrying the load under the materialist presentation. He reads that differently than I do. Chase him anyway.

**On the ritual department and when it closed.** This is the one I'd hand you first. The continuity in that section traces back to one scholar. 林富士 Lin Fu-shih,〈中國傳統醫學社群對於「祝由」療法的評價與態度：從隋唐到清代〉, "The Chinese Medical Community's Evaluation of and Attitude Toward Zhuyou Therapy, from the Sui and Tang to the Qing,"《新史學》*New History* 31.2 (2020), and the posthumous book-length version,《從正統到異端：祝由科的歷史》, *From Orthodoxy to Heterodoxy: A History of the Zhuyou Department* (三民書局 Sanmin, 2024). This is where the 咒禁 spell-and-interdiction to 書禁 written-interdiction to 祝由 incantation-therapy continuity comes from, and where the finding that most medical histories misdate it comes from. Lin's central finding is the one that matters most here: across a millennium the Chinese medical community's attitude toward zhuyou was broadly affirmative, and the hostile literature appears only after the abolition. He also finds the abolition had little to do with any conflict with orthodox medicine, and that the practice carried on regardless of the roster. Sixty-four peer-reviewed pages, by a specialist with no stake in my argument. Ying Zhang, "Combating Illness-Causing Demons in the Home," *Late Imperial China* 39.2 (2018), has the printed evidence for the carrying on. 1571 itself is standard, off 《大明會典》, the Collected Statutes of the Great Ming.

**On the talismans as licensed.** Start with the Tang job description. It doesn't say 符, talisman. 《唐六典》, the Tang administrative code, gives the 咒禁博士, the professor of incantation and interdiction, as teaching 咒禁 to expel malignant spectres, and glosses the craft with five methods: 存思 interior visualization, 禹步 the pace of Yu, 營目 eye-fixing, 掌訣 palm-formulas and 手印 mudras. All of that is body and mind, and not one of it is a written object. Sun Simiao's own preface separates them as well, listing 禁咒 and 符印, interdiction-and-incantation and talismans-and-seals, as two of five emergency arts. On the Tang text alone, the licence reads as speech and gesture. That gloss comes through the secondary literature, and the print 《唐六典》卷十四 is where to check it. Here's why I still count two. The book that survives from inside that world is Sun Simiao's 禁經, in 《千金翼方》卷二十九 and 三十, and the oath a student swears in 受禁法第二 is 「弟子某甲受持符禁之法」, the method of talisman-and-interdiction. Not incantation. Talisman. And the contents match the oath: 朱書此符, writing the talisman in cinnabar; 桃符一板...朱書板, a peach-wood talisman board written in cinnabar carrying the reign date and the demon's own name; 朱書帛素, writing on plain silk; 畫地作鬼字, drawing the ghost graph on the ground. Charmed water sits in there too, in 解穢禁水. Then the Song stopped making anybody infer it. The bureau's own preface of 1212 names it, 大方脈至書禁凡十有三科, thirteen departments from general medicine to written interdiction. A department name is not an inference. The spirit-summoning is in the same book, in 咒童子令說鬼姓字第二十二, incanting over a child so the ghost states its own name. One department, both operations. On the Tang alone the seat is arguable. The Song roster settles it.

**On White Tiger Decoction.** 白虎湯 is from the 《傷寒論》. 成無己 Cheng Wuji's 《傷寒明理論》 (twelfth century) explains the name: 白虎西方金神也，應秋而歸肺, the White Tiger is the metal spirit of the west, answering to autumn and returning to the lung. 吳崑 Wu Kun's 《醫方考》 (1584) adds 如秋金之令行，則夏火之炎息, when autumn metal takes command, summer fire's blaze dies down. In the four symbols the White Tiger rules the west and autumn, with metal, warfare and killing in its keeping.

**On the Song exam window.** For the record, the names the body leaves out. The department: 咒禁 incantation and interdiction under the Sui and Tang, 書禁 written interdiction (short for 書符禁咒) under the Song, where He Daren's preface counts the bureau's departments as 大方脈至書禁凡十有三科, thirteen from general medicine to written interdiction (the 宋史, the official Song history, gives the bureau of 熙寧九年, 1076, nine departments and names none of them), and 祝由 incantation therapy under the Yuan and Ming. 1571 is 隆慶五年; the date is also in 《中國醫學通史》. The five periods and six qi (五運六氣) entered the Su Wen through 王冰 Wang Bing's recension of 762. The point-by-the-clock methods are 子午流注 Zi Wu Liu Zhu and 靈龜八法 Ling Gui Ba Fa. The exam book's six categories: 墨義 textual meaning, 脈義 pulse, 大義 general principles, 論方 formulas, 假令 hypothetical cases, 運氣 periods and qi. The bureau's rules in the body are the Chongning regulations. The encyclopedia is the 永樂大典 Yongle Encyclopedia. 《太醫局諸科程文格》, Model Examination Papers of the Imperial Medical Bureau, gathered by 何大任 He Daren, 成安大夫特差判太醫局, whose preface is dated 嘉定五年仲秋朔旦, the first day of the eighth month of 1212, with a 原牒, the bureau's original memorandum, of the tenth month of the same year asking leave to print papers from the Bureau's thirteen specialties as models for candidates outside the capital. The text anybody reads now is a Qianlong reconstruction: the Siku editors assembled it out of the 永樂大典 and submitted it in the ninth month of 乾隆四十六年, 1781, arranging what they recovered into nine juan. 墨義 9, 脈義 6, 大義 37, 論方 8, 假令 18, 運氣 9. Eighty-seven answers, and they are anthology totals across multiple sittings and specialties, not one examination paper. Two more things you should have. The Siku 提要, the editors' summary at the head of the book, says the editors did not know who had gathered the collection, 其裒為一集不知何人所編, so the attribution to He Daren rests on the preface and the memorandum, which the Siku text itself prints at the head of the first juan. And for the load on one candidate, use the Chongning rule in the 宋史 itself: 次塲方脉試脉證運氣大義各二道, two 運氣 questions in the second session, and 鍼瘍試小經大義三道運氣大義二道, two more for the needling and sore streams. The Siku 提要 paraphrases it as 次塲方脈及臨證運氣各二道. That per-candidate figure is stronger ground than an anthology total, and it's the one the body uses. The 嘉祐 Jiayou-era promulgation of the 1050s and 60s, and Huizong writing 運氣 into the state compendium, both sit earlier and both are inference rather than examination text.

**On the Yuan roster.** The Yuan bureau set questions in thirteen departments, 十三科, in the words of the 元史, the official Yuan history (卷八十一, 選舉一), which names none of them. The lineage from 咒禁 to 祝由 is Lin's. And a roster is not the imperial examination, it's a bureau certifying its own staff, so when I say the state examined this, I mean the medical bureau, not that a man sat the 科舉, the imperial civil service examination, in exorcism.

**On the 1822 strike.** The Qing removal of the acupuncture department from the Imperial Academy of Medicine is conventionally dated 1822, on secondary sources.

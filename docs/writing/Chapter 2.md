# The Split Was the Add-On

From *The Magick That Never Left: Classical Chinese Medicine and the Tradition Still Beneath It*

*~15 minute read*

*A note before we start. This is a personal essay on the history and philosophy of Chinese medicine, and on my own spiritual practice, not medical advice. When I describe what classical doctors did (exorcism, talismans, spirits, demons), I'm reporting the historical record, not a treatment I sell. I use "magick," with the k, as a loose English umbrella for things the sources themselves keep separate (exorcism, talismanic and incantation therapy, divination, ritual), to flag the old craft and not stage tricks. It's shorthand for a general reader, not a claim that the tradition filed all of it under one word. Where the differences matter, I keep them. Anyone described here is composited; every scene is an illustration, not a record of a real person.*

---

![The character 醫 brush-inked on aged paper beside an old bronze wine vessel and a few arrowheads.](https://zacharylui.ca/images/09_yi_character.png)

Sun Simiao is the closest thing Chinese medicine has to a patron saint: the King of Medicine, later deified, still on the syllabus thirteen centuries on. Open the formulary that made him canonical and you find, a page or two apart, a forty-five-ingredient herb prescription and the incantation to be chanted over the person who takes it. Same book. Same hand. No wall between them. That is not a folk healer at the margins of medicine; that is its dead center, declining to draw the line the rest of us now treat as obvious and ancient.

That's the argument in miniature, and the romantic version of it is wrong, so let me be precise. I'm not saying medicine and magick were ever an undivided blur; the people who did both could tell a spell from a drug, and sometimes said so. I'm saying the line was optional. It sat on the table for a thousand years, and the figures at the center kept declining to draw it. I'm not quarrelling with historians, either, who settled this decades ago. The reader I'm arguing with is the one trained where I trained: the licensed acupuncturist handed a Chinese medicine scrubbed of its spirits and told the clean thing is the original, with the superstition filed off sometime back. That's the picture that's backwards.

You can read the whole reversal in one word, too — 醫, the word I practise under — but the word only confirms what the record already shows, and the record is older and harder than any glyph. The magick was never the add-on; the split was. So start where it's least arguable: in the ground.

---

## Start in the ground

![A fragment of ancient Chinese silk medical manuscript, columns of faded brush characters.](https://zacharylui.ca/images/10_mawangdui_silk.png)

Start with the thing we can actually hold. In 1973, diggers opened a tomb sealed in 168 BCE and found China's earliest medical handbooks, centuries older than the famous classics. In the main one, the cures for a single illness sit side by side: a herbal remedy, and a spell to say over it, same ailment, same page. More than thirty of the recipes are flat-out incantation and ritual, shelved right in among the drugs. And here's the part that matters: the era could tell spell-folk from drug-folk when it wanted to, so this isn't ignorance. The distinction was available. For the patient in front of you, nobody bothered to enforce it. Donald Harper, who translated these manuscripts, calls the early picture a craft "dominated by magico-religious belief and practice" that only slowly turned into theory. The mix isn't a corruption that crept into a pure medicine later. It's in the oldest evidence we can dig up, and we dug it up.

And the spirit-worker wasn't folklore squatting at the edge of medicine; he got there first. 巫 (wū), the spirit-medium, shows up in the oracle bones, the oldest Chinese writing there is, older in the record than the book-trained physician. There's no tidy English word for what a wū did, because he did all of it at once: medium, healer, exorcist, reader of signs. In that world you couldn't pull "medicine" loose from "spirit," because nobody had drawn the line yet. You gathered a herb by talking to the spirit of the plant. A needle could pull out a thorn or drive out a demon. For a long stretch he was even a recognized kind of doctor with his own name: 巫醫 (wū yī), "shaman" and "physician" at once, sometimes two people, sometimes one. You don't weld two words into one job title for things nobody ever thought belonged together.

And the word itself carries the same story, in a way that turns out to be citable rather than poetic. 醫, the word I practise under, has a second, attested spelling: 毉, which sets 巫, the shaman, where the standard form sets 酉, the wine vessel. The sinologist Michael Carr reads that 毉 as a little scene — a shaman-doctor "in the act of exorcistical healing," arrows in a quiver, a hand on a lance, and the spirit-medium himself; Paul Unschuld ties the same form to the exorcist-physician described in the Liji. And John Schiffeler reads the swap itself — shaman out, wine vessel in — as the institutional moment: healing "taken over," in his words, from the wu by the "priest-physicians" who dosed with wines and elixirs. The character, written both ways, carries the takeover the rest of this chapter documents.

One line of restraint, though, because this is the easy place to overreach. I'm not telling you 毉 is the buried "original" and 醫 a cover-up. Schuessler still traces the word's sound back to "potion," the wine jar, and the priority is genuinely contested. So let me name the seam before a hostile reader does: of my four arguments, the character is the weakest, not the strongest. The wine-jar-to-potion-to-physician root actually cuts against the shaman reading, and what carries the point isn't the glyph but the things standing around it — the oracle-bone priority of 巫 and the welded 巫醫 compound. The airtight claim is smaller and still does the work: the tradition wrote the word both ways, and the scholar who studied the swap reads it as the shaman handing off to the wine.

## "Acupuncturist" and "herbalist" are translation casualties

Now the English. "Acupuncturist." "Herbalist." Two trades, two people: that's what the words imply, and most patients never question it. Watch what they hide.

"Acupuncturist" sounds like someone whose whole job is putting needles in you. But the Chinese is 針灸 (zhēn jiǔ), two characters: 針, the needle, and 灸, moxa, burning mugwort, the fire half of the work, cautery and heat and smoke. The trade is the needle and the burning both. English kept the needle and dropped the fire — not for lack of a word, since "moxibustion" is right there in the dictionary, but because the field named itself, in English, after half its own toolkit. Shorthand, not a gap. "Herbalist" is worse: in English, somebody who sells you plants. The Chinese category is 本草 (běn cǎo), the materia medica, and it was never just plants. Minerals. Animal parts. Substances prepared closer to alchemy than gardening. And the person who handled all of it, classically, was the same person who held the needle. He was 醫, the doctor.

So here is the loss, stated precisely, because the easy version is wrong. Start with the concession: English didn't invent the division of labour. Chinese medicine had specialists for centuries; needle-work and herb-work were genuinely distinct callings, and nobody needed a translator to pull them apart. The honest charge is narrower. The old word 醫 covered a physician who needled and burned and dosed and read pulses and, when it came to it, dealt with the spirits and the dead. "Acupuncturist" and "herbalist" keep the first four jobs and quietly drop the last. The loss isn't the splitting. The splitting was already there. The loss is the piece that made it into neither box.

![A classical physician's desk: worn brush and inkstone, an open thread-bound medical book, a low candle.](https://zacharylui.ca/images/11_scholar_desk.png)

And specializing wasn't a Western sin done to a Chinese whole. The Chinese did it themselves, by law. The Yuan state medical exam was built on thirteen official subjects, the ones you'd expect, internal medicine, pediatrics, eye medicine, bone-setting, acupuncture, and one of the thirteen, with its own name and its own section, was "charms and spells" (祝由). Ritual healing wasn't whispered about at the back door. It was an examinable subject of the state academy, beside acupuncture, and the scholars closest to it, like Philip Cho, read it as serious, current medicine, not folklore. Here, again, is the shape of the whole argument: the divide was available, written into the curriculum, and the spirit-work sat on the orthodox side of it anyway. The clean cut that finally put it outside medicine came much later, and the English titles did a cruder version of that cut: they kept the needle and the herb and let the department for the spirits quietly close.

Here's the difference that matters, though, and it's not English versus Chinese. The tradition split the role on purpose and kept the meaning; English split it and lost the meaning. Across the Tang and the Yuan the imperial academy kept filing down the name of its ritual-healing office, from the "Office of charm-interdiction, zhuyou, and interdiction" (fujin zhuyou jinke) to plain zhuyou ke, the office of zhuyou. By the twelfth century its physicians had folded exorcism into that one word deliberately, to pull the popular ritual masters' techniques inside orthodox medicine, and later read zhuyou as the treatment for emotional disorders bred by "hidden vices." Philip Cho lays the whole maneuver out: the spirit-work kept, relabelled, absorbed.

Here I step off the cited record, into what I was taught and what I still hear in clinic rather than anything I can footnote — take it as oral tradition, not scholarship. The same hidden-name habit is alive in how people still talk. The "surgeon" is the exorcist; the "physician" is the one who quietly works your life issues and reads your body as a map; the "nutritionist" is the farmer-herb person. You don't say the big word, you say the small one — a little herbology, some bone-setting, going to talk to your ancestors. The surface stays polite and the work goes on underneath. It's the same move the academy made in the archive, only kept alive in the mouth instead of the catalogue.

So when somebody asks why an acupuncturist is talking about ancestors and spirits, the honest answer is that "acupuncturist" is a translation casualty, a shrunk English box around a role that was never that small. The needle doesn't do the magick. The word for it once held both.

## Sun Simiao didn't bother coding any of it

I opened on Sun Simiao, so let me make good on him, because he isn't a folk healer on the margins. He's 藥王, the King of Medicine, later deified, with temples raised to him. Tang dynasty, died 682. He wrote the two formularies that anchor the entire materia medica tradition, the 備急千金要方 and its sequel the 千金翼方: thousands of formulas, pulse diagnosis, acupuncture, moxa. Foundational clinical literature, not folklore. TCM students still study out of him today.

Here's the part the foundations class skips. The sequel also contains what scholars call his "Book of Spells": talismans, incantations, exorcisms, acupuncture points listed specifically for driving out demons. One of its rites goes after chu — "infestation," a wasting illness read as a demon that has moved into the body and settled in. The healer speaks as the "Master of Spirits" and runs the thing down in a litany: when the Master of Spirits bespells the chu, it is confused; bespells it again, it perishes; again, it ceases to exist. That is in the formulary, by the King of Medicine, a page or two from a draught for fever. The scholar who studied this material most closely thinks Sun must have trained as a Daoist priest, because only an initiate would have known the rites. And he didn't code any of it: no occult appendix in different binding, no private grimoire for initiates. He set the talisman next to the decoction because, to him, they were the same project, which was to treat the sick person in front of you with whatever the situation calls for.

This is the heart of the case, so press on it. The objection isn't that Sun couldn't have drawn the line. By the time the Yellow Emperor's classic was compiled, elite literate medicine was already drawing it, and drawing it against the wu. One Suwen passage says flatly that with someone "bound up with ghosts and spirits" you cannot even begin to talk real medicine. A skeptic reads that and says: there's your proof, the serious tradition had already cut the spirit-work loose, and what carried on was folk superstition the literati had outgrown. That reading has teeth, and I'll grant it the strong form. The classics drew the line themselves, early.

Here's why it still doesn't carry. The line got drawn, and then the people at the center stepped over it on purpose. Sun, the most canonical doctor the tradition has, read the Suwen and still put the spells in his own formulary. Centuries on, the Yuan academy read the same misgivings and still listed ritual healing among its thirteen subjects, beside acupuncture, on the licensing roster. The Suwen's distaste and zhuyou's place on the state exam are both true at once: two threads twisted into one rope, arguing the whole way down, neither one throwing the other out. The clean, total cut comes only with the modern reform — the twentieth-century remaking of Chinese medicine into a secular, standardized profession, which is the story of chapter 1. This chapter only fixes the before.

![A cinnabar-red paper talisman, one corner burned to ash, beside a dark bowl of herbal decoction and dried mugwort.](https://zacharylui.ca/images/12_talisman_decoction.png)

It wasn't a one-man quirk, either. In the ritual manuals the spell and the herbs are a single move: you write a charm in red ink on yellow paper, burn it to ash, and swallow the ash in a decoction picked to match the charm. Spell over the medicine, medicine carrying the spell. And Sun opens his whole work not with technique but with the doctor's character: a still heart, real compassion, the same care for the rich man, the poor man, and even the enemy, no appetite for money or fame, and the duty to work on yourself before you're fit to touch anyone. The most important medical text in the tradition opens by telling you that you, the doctor, are the instrument, and an untuned instrument plays flat. He says that first, before a single formula.

## Why this matters away from the library

I can hear the objection, because I've made it to myself. The etymology's a hook, the thirteen subjects are a good fact, Sun Simiao's a party trick, but it's all centuries dead. Does any of it change anything once a living person is in the room?

Here's the honest answer, and I'll give the hedge once and not keep apologizing for it: this is an observation about attention, not a claim about outcomes. I'm not telling you a ritual cures a disease.

A patient comes in for insomnia. The narrow practitioner, the one "acupuncturist" describes, takes the complaint at its word: points for sleep, a calming formula, book the follow-up. The one who still carries the older role hears "I can't sleep" as the first line of a longer story and asks the next question. What's keeping you up. What changed this year. What you haven't said out loud to anyone. Same training, same needles, same hands — but one of them has somewhere to put the grief or the dread sitting under the symptom, and the other has an intake form with no box for it. You cannot work with what you never let yourself look at.

None of this is a reason to drop the needle and wave incense instead; that's the opposite ditch, and people fall into it just as hard. Acupuncture and Chinese herbal medicine do real work inside the scope they're studied and regulated for. The claim is smaller and harder to dodge: the needle and the spirit-work came down one tradition that kept refusing to wall them off, and the practitioner who still holds both asks more of the encounter than the one handed only the narrow half. Not curing more. Asking more.

Two eyes, one face — one the technician's, one the spirit-worker's, set in the same head. (It's the image chapter 1 turned on.) The old doctors used both and didn't think it exotic. They were just looking at the person in front of them with the whole face the medicine came with. The magick was never the add-on; the split was.

## Want to dig into the actual record

Same deal as last chapter. Here's where the claims come from, so you can check my work.

For the word and the graph, the rigorous source is Axel Schuessler's [*ABC Etymological Dictionary of Old Chinese*](https://uhpress.hawaii.edu/title/abc-etymological-dictionary-of-old-chinese/) (University of Hawai'i Press): it gives the root sense of 醫 (yī) as "potion," then "physician," and it traces 巫 (wū), "shaman, spirit medium, magician," back to the oracle bones. I lean on it precisely to stay out of the folk-etymology trap; the word for doctor does not "mean" shaman, and I say so. The variant graph 毉, which sets 巫 (shaman) where 醫 sets 酉 (wine vessel), is read by Michael Carr ("Shamanic *Heng* 恆 'Constancy'," [*Review of Liberal Arts*](http://hdl.handle.net/10252/1754) 83, 1992, p. 117) as a depiction of a shaman-doctor "in the act of exorcistical healing," and by Paul U. Unschuld (*Medicine in China: A History of Ideas*, University of California Press, 1985, p. 37) as the exorcist-physician of the *Liji*; John William Schiffeler ("The Origin of Chinese Folk Medicine," [*Asian Folklore Studies*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1177648) 35:1, 1976, p. 27) reads the substitution of wine for shaman as medicine being "taken over … by the 'priest-physicians.'" I use the swap as the argument, not as a priority claim — Schuessler's sound-etymology leaves open which form came first. For shaman and doctor as one named historical role, Christopher Cullen and Vivienne Lo's [*Medieval Chinese Medicine*](https://www.routledge.com/Medieval-Chinese-Medicine-The-Dunhuang-Medical-Manuscripts/Cullen-Lo/p/book/9780415342957) discusses the "physician-shaman" (yīwū, 巫醫) and the "spirit specialist" as recognized categories, and records the medieval Daoist and Buddhist attacks on those figures, which is where the "the divide was available, and Sun declined it" point comes from.

For the earliest hard evidence, Donald Harper's [*Early Chinese Medical Literature*](https://www.routledge.com/Early-Chinese-Medical-Literature/Harper/p/book/9781138968080) translates the Mawangdui manuscripts (tomb sealed 168 BCE) and documents the more than thirty exorcistic-incantation recipes interspersed with drug recipes in the *Recipes for Fifty-Two Ailments*, the wuyi 巫醫 "shaman-physician" compound in Warring States and Han usage, and the summary that early Chinese medicine was "an archaic craft dominated by magico-religious belief and practice" that only gradually became theory-driven. It's the closest thing to a smoking gun for the "before" picture, and it's a primary-manuscript source from the field's authority on it.

For Sun Simiao, Michel Strickmann's [*Chinese Magical Medicine*](https://www.sup.org/books/asian-studies/chinese-magical-medicine) (Stanford University Press) is the definitive treatment: it walks through Sun's "Book of Spellbinding" (禁經) in the two closing chapters of the Qianjin Yifang, the ritual therapy meant to "enhance his use of drugs, moxa, and acupuncture," the incantations filed by ailment, the chu demon-infestations with the healer speaking as "Master of Spirits," and the circumstantial evidence that Sun was trained as a Taoist priest. The *Da Yi Jing Cheng* (大醫精誠), his opening ethical chapter, is catalogued and translated in the [*Routledge Handbook of Chinese Medicine*](https://www.routledge.com/Routledge-Handbook-of-Chinese-Medicine/Lo-Stanley-Baker/p/book/9781032149776) (Nathan Sivin's rendering, "The perfect integrity of the great physician"); Volker Scheid's *Chinese Medicine in Contemporary China* also points to Unschuld's translation of Sun's treatise on the "great physician" (dayi, 大醫). And the *Routledge Handbook* makes the larger point for me: in Sun's own diagnostic writing "the difference between the skills of doctor and diviner was subtle," the two trades genuinely entwined rather than walled off.

For the official standing of ritual healing, two academic sources carry the weight. Reiko Shinno's [*The Politics of Chinese Medicine Under Mongol Rule*](https://www.routledge.com/The-Politics-of-Chinese-Medicine-Under-Mongol-Rule/Shinno/p/book/9781138099326) (Routledge) documents the Yuan state curriculum of thirteen medical subjects (shisan ke 十三科), with "charms and spells" (zhuyou shujin ke 祝由書禁科) listed as its own examinable specialty beside acupuncture. Philip S. Cho's [*Healing and Ritual Imagination in Chinese Medicine*](https://brill.com/view/journals/east/38/1/article-p71_5.xml) (in *East Asian Science, Technology, and Medicine*) studies zhuyou as orthodox, cutting-edge medicine for the post-Northern-Song elite, and traces how the imperial academy renamed the ritual office from fujin zhuyou jinke down to zhuyou ke and reread exorcism as the treatment of emotional disorders. For the talisman-and-decoction practice itself, the *Routledge Handbook of Chinese Medicine* again, Luis Fernando Bernardi Junqueira's chapter "Numinous herbs," working straight from the talismanic manuals where spells are burned to ash and taken with herbal decoctions and the Heavenly Physician is invoked to bless the herbs. For the *Suwen*'s own pushback on spirit-etiology, Amy Chang's [*A Field Guide to the Huangdi Neijing Suwen*](https://us.singingdragon.com/products/a-field-guide-to-the-hungd-nijing-swn) (Singing Dragon, with Sabine Wilms) supplies the passage and the reading. And for the larger picture of how medicine in early China took shape among the divinatory and cosmological arts, Geoffrey Lloyd and Nathan Sivin's [*The Way and the Word*](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300101607/the-way-and-the-word/) (Yale University Press) is the standard comparative study.

And the throughline from chapter 1 — how the modern Chinese state remade this medicine into a secular profession and pushed the spirit-work out — still holds: Sean Hsiang-lin Lei's [*Neither Donkey nor Horse: Medicine in the Struggle over China's Modernity*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/N/bo16968081.html) (University of Chicago Press) and Volker Scheid's [*Chinese Medicine in Contemporary China: Plurality and Synthesis*](https://www.dukeupress.edu/chinese-medicine-in-contemporary-china) (Duke University Press) are the books closest to my framing on what got de-centred, and when. This chapter is just the before-picture of the same story.

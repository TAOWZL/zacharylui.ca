Two things to hold. The Han Feizi is a philosophical text making a point about Laozi, not a philologist's derivation, and modern scholarship treats it as folk etymology. I use it for what the tradition took itself to be doing. And 相 and 象 are not cognates. Separate words, homophones in modern Mandarin only. The distinction I draw at the end of that item, that a talisman makes a link rather than finding one, is mine and it is a reading, not a finding.

**On the illumination item.** The two chapters are 大醫習業 and 大醫精誠, and they open juan one of Sun Simiao's 備急千金要方, ahead of the formulas. 大醫精誠 is the famous one, usually rendered "On the Absolute Sincerity of Great Physicians," and it is the text people reach for when they want a Chinese medical ethics. I am working from the Chinese and from the standard accounts of the chapter's placement, not from a print edition in my hands. For the long form of the practice, 養生 nourishing life and 內丹 internal alchemy, Fabrizio Pregadio is the standard scholar on the alchemical side and Vivienne Lo has worked the nourishing-life material in the excavated manuscripts. And the claim that the standardized curriculum demoted cultivation from practice to coursework is mine, off my own training and my own teaching, not a finding from the literature.

**On the banishing item.** 張從正 Zhang Congzheng, styled 子和 Zihe, c. 1156 to 1228. His book is the 《儒門事親》 *Rumen shiqin*, Confucians Serve Their Parents, and no complete English translation of it has been published, so the two lines I quote are my own renderings off the Chinese, both from 〈汗下吐三法該盡治病詮〉, the essay arguing that sweating, purging and vomiting exhaust the treatment of disease: 夫病之一物，非人身素有之也。或自外而入，或由内而生，皆邪氣也, and 邪去而元氣自復. It's item thirteen in the standard fifteen-juan text, and because the items are numbered straight through the whole book, thirteen falls third inside 卷二, between 攻裏發表寒熱殊塗箋十二 and 凡在上者皆可吐式十四. The received text reads 邪去而元氣自復也 and I've dropped the final 也. On the school label, be careful with me. 攻邪, attacking evil, is what Chinese reference literature keyed to 《中医各家学说》, Doctrines of the Various Schools of Chinese Medicine, calls the current. English-language writing splits: the *Journal of Chinese Medicine* says Attack and Drain School, the *Journal of Traditional Chinese Medical Sciences* says school of purgation, and I'm following the second, and Zhang is standardly called its 代表人物, its representative figure, rather than its founder. The larger caution is that the whole "four great masters of the Jin and Yuan" grouping was assembled later: Fabien Simonis, "Illness, Texts, and 'Schools' in Danxi Medicine," in Benjamin A. Elman, ed., *Antiquarianism, Language, and Medical Philology* (Brill, 2015), traces how the set was built in stages, with Zhang Congzheng substituted into it in the seventeenth century by 張璐. So I use the label the field uses, and you should know it was applied backwards. A short peer-reviewed English note exists: Huang and Liang, "Zhang Congzheng, physician and initiator of the 'school of purgation'," *Journal of Traditional Chinese Medical Sciences* 5:4 (2018), 321-322.

**On expulsion as a category, and who got there before me.** Philip S. Cho, "Healing and Ritual Imagination in Chinese Medicine: The Multiple Interpretations of Zhuyou," *East Asian Science, Technology, and Medicine* 38 (2013), and his 2005 Penn dissertation on zhuyou exorcism, are the closest existing work to this. What none of them argues, and what I'm extending, is that the ordinary pharmacological and needling repertoire runs the same operation. Take that last step as mine. And the reading that clearing and settling are one move rather than two, that's my synthesis off Zhang's sentence, not something I can hand you out of a journal.

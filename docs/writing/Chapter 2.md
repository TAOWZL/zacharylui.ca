# The Magick Was Never the Add-On

Most people think the magick got bolted onto Chinese medicine somewhere along the way, some folk-superstition that crept in around the edges of the real clinical work. It's the other way around. The oldest medical handbooks we've ever dug up put the herbal cure and the spell to say over it on the same page. Sun Simiao, the most canonical physician the tradition has, wrote spells and herb formulas in the same book and didn't think he was changing the subject. Here's how classical Chinese medicine still sits underneath the language we use for it.

From *The Magick That Never Left: Classical Chinese Medicine and the Tradition Still Beneath It*

*A quick note before we start. This is a personal essay about the history and philosophy of Chinese medicine, and about my own spiritual practice, not medical advice. When I describe what classical doctors did (exorcism, talismans, spirits, demons), I'm reporting the historical record, not describing a treatment I sell. The history below didn't separate medicine from magick; I'm not asking you to fuse them in a clinic today, only to see that they began as one thing. And one word on a word. I use "magick," with the k, as a modern English umbrella for a bundle of things the sources themselves keep separate: exorcism, talismanic and incantation therapy, divination, ritual, and the correspondence cosmology under them. The k just flags that I mean the old craft, not stage tricks. It's my shorthand for a general reader, though, not a claim that the tradition filed all of this under one word, or that a Western category drops cleanly onto Han or Tang China. Where the differences matter, I keep them. Names and details of anyone described here are changed or composited. Every scene is an illustration, not a record of a real person.*

![The character 醫 brush-inked on aged paper beside an old bronze wine vessel and a few arrowheads.](https://zacharylui.ca/images/09_yi_character.png)

The quickest way to see what this whole chapter is about is to look at one word. The word for the thing I do.

A practitioner of Chinese medicine writes it without thinking, the way you sign your own name without watching your hand: 醫. One character. It's on the clinic sign, it's the second half of 中醫, "Chinese medicine," it's the entire profession folded into a single glyph. And almost nobody who writes it, including most of the people licensed to, has ever stopped to read what's actually in there.

Because it isn't one thing. Pull the character apart and you get a stack of jobs a modern eye would never file under one heading: a hand gripping a weapon, a vessel of medicinal wine, and a shaman standing behind both. A cutter, a druggist, and a spirit-worker, bundled into one sign and treated as too obviously a single trade to need explaining.

That bundle is the argument. What an outside observer would split on sight into "the medicine part" and "the magick part" was never two things that later got mixed together. It was one thing that later got cut apart. And once the words did the cutting, everyone downstream assumed the pieces had always been separate. They weren't. So let me start where the splitting started, which is the language itself.

---

## Start with the word

Here's a thing most people never look at, practitioners included. The Chinese word for medicine, for the physician, for the whole enterprise, is 醫 (yī). It's the character on the clinic sign. It's the 醫 in 中醫, "Chinese medicine." Crack it open and look at how it's built: the bottom is 酉, a wine vessel, the radical for alcohol and fermented things. The top is a quiver of arrows and a hand gripping a weapon. So the standard character for "doctor" is already a little picture of someone pulling arrows out of a wound and reaching for the medicinal wine. Surgery and pharmacy, baked right into the glyph.

Now I have to be careful here, because this is where it's easy to overreach. I'm not going to tell you the word secretly means "shaman." It doesn't. The best etymology we have, from the Old Chinese dictionaries, traces 醫 back to "potion," and from there "physician." The root really is the medicinal drink, the wine jar at the bottom. So far, ordinary.

But here's the part that holds. The shaman is right there in the same world, and he got there first. 巫 (wū), the word for the spirit-medium, turns up in the oracle bones, the oldest Chinese writing there is, older in the record than the book-trained doctor. And there's no tidy English word for what a wū did, because he did all of it at once: medium, healer, exorcist, reader of signs. In that world you couldn't pull "medicine" loose from "spirit," because nobody had drawn the line yet. You gathered a herb by talking to the spirit of the plant. A needle could pull out a thorn or drive out a demon. Healing the body and dealing with the dead were the same conversation.

Even a skeptic sees it in the character. Charles Buck, who has no patience for romanticizing this stuff, still reads a shaman into the glyph of 醫: the old sign of the shaman's sticks, rattled to drive off bad spirits, sitting next to the arrows and the wine. He's a clinician, not a language scholar, so take that as a hint, not a proof. The real proof isn't in the character anyway. It's in the record, and the record is not subtle.

![A fragment of ancient Chinese silk medical manuscript, columns of faded brush characters.](https://zacharylui.ca/images/10_mawangdui_silk.png)

Start with the oldest thing we can actually hold. In 1973, diggers opened a tomb sealed in 168 BCE and found China's earliest medical handbooks, centuries older than the famous classics. In the main one, the cures for a single illness sit side by side: a herbal remedy, and a spell to say over it, same ailment, same page. More than thirty of the recipes are flat-out incantation and ritual, shelved right in among the drugs. The sources of that era do sometimes tell the spell-folk apart from the drug-folk, so it isn't that nobody could draw the line. It's that in the handbook, for the patient in front of you, nobody bothered to: the spell and the drug sit side by side, no wall between them. Donald Harper, who translated these manuscripts, sums up the early picture as a craft "dominated by magico-religious belief and practice" that only slowly turned into a theory. So the mix isn't a corruption that crept into a pure medicine later. It's the floor the whole thing was built on, and we dug it up.

And the shaman wasn't only a brushstroke in a character. For a long stretch he was a recognized kind of doctor with his own name. The early sources use one compound word, 巫醫 (wū yī), that means "shaman" and "physician" at the same time, sometimes two people working together, sometimes one person who was both. You don't weld two words into a single job title for things nobody ever thought belonged together.

So before we touch a single clinical claim, the language is already telling on itself. The word for the medicine I practise has a shaman at the bottom of it, painted over with a wine jar. Most of my colleagues have never noticed he's still down there. That's not their fault (nobody teaches the character this way), but he's sitting in the word every one of us writes on the door.

## "Acupuncturist" and "herbalist" are translation casualties

Now look at the words we reach for in English, because they've been quietly doing something. "Acupuncturist." "Herbalist." Two separate trades, two separate people. That's what the English implies, and most patients never think to question it. But watch what those words are hiding.

"Acupuncturist." In English that sounds like a person whose whole job is sticking needles in you. Needle-sticker. But the Chinese is 針灸 (zhēn jiǔ), and that's two characters, not one. 針 is the needle, sure. 灸 is moxa, burning mugwort, the fire half of the work: cautery, heat, smoke. The real term for the trade is needle-and-fire. English took a two-character word that's half about fire and rendered it "acupuncture," which carries the needle and drops the fire on the floor. Before you get anywhere near cosmology, the English title is already doing less than the Chinese one. It burned off half the name.

"Herbalist" is worse. In English that's somebody who sells you plants, a wall of jars. The Chinese category is 本草 (běn cǎo), "root-and-herb," the materia medica, and the classical materia medica was never just plants. It's minerals. It's animal parts. It's substances prepared in ways closer to alchemy than gardening, chosen by correspondence as much as by chemistry. And the person who handled all that, classically, wasn't a different guy from the needle person. He was 醫, the doctor, the one with the shaman at the bottom of his name. One role.

That's what the English really buries. "Acupuncturist" and "herbalist" aren't just narrow translations. They're a partition. They take one undivided role, 醫, the physician who needled and burned and dosed and read pulses and, when it came to it, dealt with the spirits and the dead, and they slice it into two technicians who each do one trick.

![A classical physician's desk: worn brush and inkstone, an open thread-bound medical book, a low candle.](https://zacharylui.ca/images/11_scholar_desk.png)

Now, splitting medicine into specialties isn't some modern Western sin. The Chinese did it themselves, by law. Under the Yuan dynasty, the state medical exam, the one you had to pass to practise, was built on thirteen official subjects. Internal medicine, pediatrics, eye medicine, bone-setting, acupuncture, the ones you'd expect. And one of the thirteen, a subject in its own right, was "charms and spells." Sit with that. Ritual healing wasn't whispered about at the back door. It was an examinable subject of the state medical academy, on the same exam roster as acupuncture, and the scholars who've looked closest at it, like Philip Cho, argue it was treated as serious, current medicine, not folklore. The magic wasn't sneaking in through a window. It was on the exam.

So the lesson isn't that splitting is bad. It's that for most of this history, when they split medicine up, the spirit work was one of the pieces: a named, official specialty inside medicine, not a superstition quarantined outside it. The partition that finally cut the magick *out* came much later. And the English titles I inherited did a cruder version of that later cut: they took one role that had a wū underneath it and handed back two technicians, with no department left for the spirits at all.

And honestly, Chinese did a version of this to itself long before English showed up, except it did it on purpose, and kept the meaning. The culture is full of code words for exactly this work. The "surgeon" was the exorcist. The "physician" was the one who actually worked your life issues and read your body as a map. The "nutritionist" was the farmer-herb person. You don't say the big obvious word; you say the quiet one. Oh, you're going to do a little herbology. You're going for some bone-setting. You're going to go talk to your ancestors. Nobody announces what's actually happening. It's an indirect reference, a polite surface with a different thing underneath. The shamanic work never stopped. It got a respectable label stapled over it and, under the label, kept right on developing. So when English came along and shrank 醫 into "acupuncturist" and "herbalist," it wasn't inventing the trick of the hidden name. It was doing a clumsier, lossier version of a move the tradition had always made, and losing the part the Chinese code words had carefully kept.

So when somebody asks, "you're an acupuncturist, why are you talking about ancestors and tiles and spirits," the honest answer is that "acupuncturist" is a translation casualty, a shrunk-down English box around a role that was never that small. I'm not saying the needle does the magick. I'm saying the *word* for it once held both, and somewhere between the imperial academy and my business card, the department for the spirits got closed and the sign taken down.

## Sun Simiao didn't bother coding any of it

Here's where I send anyone who thinks the magick is some fringe thing the tradition is embarrassed about. Go read Sun Simiao. Working with spirits, being a shaman, being a physician. Historically that was one thing, one person, and Sun Simiao is the cleanest proof we have, because he isn't a folk healer on the margins. He's the center.

Sun Simiao is not fringe. He's 藥王, the King of Medicine, later deified, with temples to him, the single most canonical physician in the whole tradition. Tang dynasty, died 682 (the birth year's argued; some say 581, some push it to 541; either way he reportedly lived past 100). If Chinese medicine has a patron saint, it's this man. He wrote the two books that anchor the entire materia medica tradition: the 備急千金要方, *Essential Formulas Worth a Thousand in Gold for Emergencies*, and its sequel the 千金翼方, the supplement. Thousands of formulas, hundreds of substances, pulse diagnosis, acupuncture, moxa, the works. This is foundational clinical literature, not folklore. TCM students still study out of him today.

Here's the part they skip in the foundations class. The sequel to his great formula book, the Qianjin Yifang, also contains what scholars call his "Book of Spells." Talismans. Incantations. Exorcisms. Acupuncture points listed specifically for driving out demons. The same book, by the same most-respected-doctor-in-the-tradition, gives you a forty-five-ingredient herb formula on one page and the spell to chant over it on the next. The scholar who studied this material most closely thinks Sun must have trained as a Daoist priest, because only an initiate would have known the rites. Confucian classics, Daoist alchemy, Buddhist ethics, spells, and herbs, all in one man who didn't think he was juggling five jobs.

And the part I keep circling back to: he didn't bother coding any of it. There's no "occult appendix" hidden in the back with different binding. He didn't write the respectable medicine in one book and quarantine the spells in a private grimoire for initiates. He set the talisman next to the decoction because, to him, they were the same project: treat the sick person in front of you with whatever the situation calls for. The thick line we'd draw, medicine on one side, magick on the other, he simply doesn't draw.

And it's not that he didn't know the line could be drawn. It was being drawn all around him. The Yellow Emperor's classic itself takes a swing at spirit-blaming: it says, in plain effect, that with someone "bound up with ghosts and spirits" you can't even begin to talk real medicine. From the religious side, Daoist and Buddhist writers were slamming the spirit-healers as con men after your money. So the option to wall the magic off and keep only the respectable part was right there on the table. Sun, the most canonical physician in the whole tradition, looked at that option and said no. With every reason to leave the spells out of his medical encyclopedia, he put them in. That's the whole point. It wasn't that nobody could imagine the divide. It's that the man at the center refused to use it.

![A cinnabar-red paper talisman, one corner burned to ash, beside a dark bowl of herbal decoction and dried mugwort.](https://zacharylui.ca/images/12_talisman_decoction.png)

And this wasn't a one-man quirk. In the ritual healing manuals, the spell and the herbs are a single move, not two. You write a charm in red ink on yellow paper, burn it to ash, and swallow the ash with a herbal brew, the brew picked to match the charm. Spell over the medicine, medicine carrying the spell. You will not find a cleaner picture of magic and pharmacy as one operation, and the people who handed it down plainly never thought they were doing two separate things.

Then look at how he opens the whole thing. The first real chapter isn't about technique at all. It's about the doctor's character. The great physician, he writes, has to come with a still heart and real compassion, has to treat the rich man and the poor man and even his enemy exactly alike, can't be in it for money or fame, and has to work on himself before he's fit to touch anyone. That's not bedside manners. The most important medical text in the tradition opens by telling you that *you*, the doctor, are the instrument, and an instrument that hasn't been tuned plays flat. He says that first. Before a single formula.

So when modern people frame the magick as the suspect add-on and the herbs-and-needles as the legitimate core, they've got the layering exactly upside down. In Sun Simiao the cultivation and the ritual aren't bolted onto the medicine. They're the floor it stands on, the same way the wū is the floor under the character. The herbs are the part that got kept. The floor is the part that got quietly pulled up later.

Let me be precise, because the precise version is the one that survives. I'm not saying Chinese medicine *is* only magick, or that nobody ever tried to separate the two. The opposite. There were always two threads, the spirit side and the plain practical side, twisted together, and people argued about the mix the entire way through. Even the scholars who track this most closely say physicians were slowly pulling away from the shamans, in status and in style, and still didn't drop the magic. Two threads, one rope. The honest history is that the magick was never the bolt-on. It was argued with, never thrown out, and stayed on the official books for roughly a thousand years. The clean, total cut, the one that finally pulls the floor out, is a modern job, and that's the story of chapter 1. This chapter only wants to nail down the before: the spirit work was holding weight, not decoration, and nobody back then had to hide it, because there was nothing yet to hide it from.

## Why this matters away from the library

I can hear the objection, because I've made it to myself. Fine. The etymology's neat, the thirteen branches are a good fact, Sun Simiao's a fun party trick. But it's all centuries dead. Does any of it change anything once a living person is in front of you?

Yes, though not as a treatment claim, so let me say plainly what I do and don't mean. I'm not telling you a ritual cures a disease. What changes is *attention*. When you hold the work the way the language and the canon held it, as one undivided thing rather than a trade with bolt-on extras, you notice differently. That's a fact about the practitioner, not a promise about an outcome.

Picture the difference plainly. Someone comes in with one stated question, the thing that fits on the line of a form. A practitioner who holds the work as a narrow service answers that question and stops: the presenting item, the protocol, done. A practitioner who holds it the way the old word holds it keeps looking past the line on the form to the whole field around the person, the pattern under the pattern, the part of the story the intake sheet has no box for. Same training, same techniques, same hands. The only difference is the size of the role each one believes he's in, and the size of the role decides what he lets himself notice. I won't dress that up as a promise about outcomes, because I can't, and the research isn't there. What I'll say is narrower and, I think, obvious: you cannot work with what you never let yourself see, and the wider frame lets you see more of what's actually in the room.

That's a practitioner's observation, not a clinical trial, and I'll say that every time, because it's true and the self-report bias is real. But it's the consistent pattern I notice, and it lines up with what the tradition was built to do before it got partitioned. The practitioner who only ever learned "acupuncturist," the shrunk English box, is working from a smaller picture of the role than the one Sun Simiao described, and a lot of the time he can feel something's missing and has no name for it, because the name got translated off his business card.

None of this means you ditch the needle and wave incense around. That's the other ditch, and people fall in it just as hard. Acupuncture and Chinese herbal medicine do real work inside the scope they're regulated and studied for. I'm not running my own profession down, and I'm not promising anybody a miracle either. The claim is narrower, and I think harder to dodge: the technique and the magick came out of one source (one character, one canon, one undivided role), and the practitioner who still remembers that is standing in something fuller than the one who forgot. Not necessarily curing more. Understanding more of what he's standing in. The magick was never the add-on. The split was the add-on. The magick is what was there first.

Two eyes, one face, like I said in chapter 1. You can work the needle eye your whole career and get plenty done. But the other eye was always open too. The old doctors used both, and they didn't think they were doing anything exotic when they did. They were just looking at the person in front of them with the whole face the medicine came with.

## Want to dig into the actual record

Same deal as last chapter. Here's where the claims come from, so you can check my work.

For the word and the graph, the rigorous source is Axel Schuessler's [*ABC Etymological Dictionary of Old Chinese*](https://uhpress.hawaii.edu/title/abc-etymological-dictionary-of-old-chinese/) (University of Hawai'i Press): it gives the root sense of 醫 (yī) as "potion," then "physician," and it traces 巫 (wū), "shaman, spirit medium, magician," back to the oracle bones. I lean on it precisely to stay out of the folk-etymology trap, the word for doctor does not "mean" shaman, and I say so in the text. Charles Buck's [*Acupuncture and Chinese Medicine: Roots of Modern Practice*](https://us.singingdragon.com/products/acupuncture-and-chinese-medicine) (Singing Dragon) reads a shaman element into the glyph itself, which I use as colour, not proof, since he is a clinician rather than a palaeographer. For shaman and doctor as one named historical role, Christopher Cullen and Vivienne Lo's [*Medieval Chinese Medicine*](https://www.routledge.com/Medieval-Chinese-Medicine-The-Dunhuang-Medical-Manuscripts/Cullen-Lo/p/book/9780415342957) discusses the "physician-shaman" (yīwū, 巫醫) and the "spirit specialist" as recognized categories, and it also records the medieval Daoist and Buddhist attacks on those figures, which is where the "the divide was available, and Sun declined it" point comes from.

For the earliest hard evidence, Donald Harper's [*Early Chinese Medical Literature*](https://www.routledge.com/Early-Chinese-Medical-Literature/Harper/p/book/9781138968080) translates the Mawangdui manuscripts (tomb sealed 168 BCE) and documents the more than thirty exorcistic-incantation recipes interspersed with drug recipes in the *Recipes for Fifty-Two Ailments*, the wuyi 巫醫 "shaman-physician" compound in Warring States and Han usage, and the summary that early Chinese medicine was "an archaic craft dominated by magico-religious belief and practice" that only gradually became theory-driven. It's the closest thing to a smoking gun for the "before" picture, and it's a primary-manuscript source from the field's authority on it.

For Sun Simiao, Michel Strickmann's [*Chinese Magical Medicine*](https://www.sup.org/books/asian-studies/chinese-magical-medicine) (Stanford University Press) is the definitive treatment: it walks through Sun's "Book of Spellbinding" (禁經) in the two closing chapters of the Qianjin Yifang: the ritual therapy meant to "enhance his use of drugs, moxa, and acupuncture," the incantations filed by ailment, the chu demon-infestations with the healer speaking as "Master of Spirits," and the circumstantial evidence that Sun was trained as a Taoist priest. The *Da Yi Jing Cheng* (大醫精誠), his opening ethical chapter, is catalogued and translated in the [*Routledge Handbook of Chinese Medicine*](https://www.routledge.com/Routledge-Handbook-of-Chinese-Medicine/Lo-Stanley-Baker/p/book/9781032149776) (Nathan Sivin's rendering, "The perfect integrity of the great physician"); Volker Scheid's *Chinese Medicine in Contemporary China* also points to Unschuld's translation of Sun's treatise on the "great physician" (dayi, 大醫). And the *Routledge Handbook* makes the larger point for me: in Sun's own diagnostic writing "the difference between the skills of doctor and diviner was subtle," the two trades genuinely entwined rather than walled off.

For the official standing of ritual healing, two academic sources carry the weight. Reiko Shinno's [*The Politics of Chinese Medicine Under Mongol Rule*](https://www.routledge.com/The-Politics-of-Chinese-Medicine-Under-Mongol-Rule/Shinno/p/book/9781138099326) (Routledge) documents the Yuan state curriculum of thirteen medical subjects (shisan ke 十三科), with "charms and spells" (zhuyou shujin ke 祝由書禁科) listed as its own examinable specialty beside acupuncture. Philip S. Cho's [*Healing and Ritual Imagination in Chinese Medicine*](https://brill.com/view/journals/east/38/1/article-p71_5.xml) (in *East Asian Science, Technology, and Medicine*) studies zhuyou as orthodox, cutting-edge medicine for the post-Northern-Song elite. For the talisman-and-decoction practice itself, the *Routledge Handbook of Chinese Medicine* again, Luis Fernando Bernardi Junqueira's chapter "Numinous herbs," working straight from the talismanic manuals where spells are burned to ash and taken with herbal decoctions and the Heavenly Physician is invoked to bless the herbs. For the *Suwen*'s own pushback on spirit-etiology, Amy Chang's [*A Field Guide to the Huangdi Neijing Suwen*](https://us.singingdragon.com/products/a-field-guide-to-the-hungd-nijing-swn) (Singing Dragon, with Sabine Wilms) supplies the passage and the reading. And for the larger picture of how medicine in early China took shape among the divinatory and cosmological arts, Geoffrey Lloyd and Nathan Sivin's [*The Way and the Word*](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300101607/the-way-and-the-word/) (Yale University Press) is the standard comparative study.

And the throughline from chapter 1 still holds: Sean Hsiang-lin Lei's [*Neither Donkey nor Horse: Medicine in the Struggle over China's Modernity*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/N/bo16968081.html) (University of Chicago Press) and Volker Scheid's [*Chinese Medicine in Contemporary China: Plurality and Synthesis*](https://www.dukeupress.edu/chinese-medicine-in-contemporary-china) (Duke University Press) are the books closest to my framing on what got de-centred, and when. This chapter is just the before-picture of the same story.

The apparatus for this chapter. The twentieth-century history is with chapter one; the ritual department, the 1571 abolition and the dating apparatus are with chapter three.

On the definition and the machinery. Start here, if you want the resonance argument at the source. The Huainanzi translation I'm quoting is John S. Major, Sarah A. Queen, Andrew Seth Meyer and Harold D. Roth, The Huainanzi (Columbia University Press, 2010), and the tuned-strings passage is chapter 6, 覽冥訓, Surveying Obscurities. Want the ganying argument on its own? Charles Le Blanc's Huai-nan Tzu: Philosophical Synthesis in Early Han Thought, The Idea of Resonance (Kan-Ying) (Hong Kong University Press, 1985), that's the standard monograph. John B. Henderson's The Development and Decline of Chinese Cosmology (Columbia University Press, 1984) is the history of correlative cosmology, as a cross-domain system. And note his title. Decline. G. E. R. Lloyd and Nathan Sivin's The Way and the Word (Yale University Press, 2002), that's the standard comparative study of how medicine, cosmos and state fit together in early China. One thing you should know about, before you take my side of it. The Han imperial bibliography filed 方技, the medical arts, and 數術, the calculational arts, in separate divisions. That's a classification my reading argues past, and you're entitled to hold it against me.

On where the categories come from, and check this against the books. Peter Carroll, Liber Null & Psychonaut (Weiser), is where the working vocabulary gets set. Gnosis, sigils and the banishing ritual are all in the student grade, Liber MMM, and the operations are the headings of Liber LUX: gnosis, evocation, invocation, divination, enchantment. The sigil method he teaches is Austin Osman Spare's, and Carroll says so. One correction to make against myself, because I had the wrong book. The tidy five-fold split of divination, enchantment, evocation, invocation and illumination isn't in Liber Null at all. It's Carroll's later Liber KKK, printed as an appendix to Liber Kaos (1992), where each of the five gets run across five levels. Andrieh Vitimus, Hands-On Chaos Magic (Llewellyn, 2009), is where the energy-body work sits, four chapters of it, alongside the cognitive-science and NLP framing and the Ovayki current. He works the sigil material at length, and as inherited technique rather than his own. Vitimus is my teacher, so treat me as an interested party. Belief taken up as a tool is the chaos position itself and it belongs to both of them.

On the anthropology the list borrows from. James Frazer's The Golden Bough for sympathetic magic and its two branches, similarity and contagion. His definition of the second, in the abridged edition: things which have once been in contact with each other continue to act on each other at a distance after the physical contact has been severed. Read now mostly as a historical document, and still the source of the framework I just ran this medicine through. Marcel Mauss and Henri Hubert, A General Theory of Magic (the 1902 Esquisse, Routledge edition), for mana as an impersonal power an operator works with. Otto and Stausberg's Defining Magic: A Reader (Equinox) collects the whole argument. And here is the objection you'll meet if you take this to a specialist, so meet it here first. The field dropped Frazer's vocabulary on purpose, and it works with the indigenous terms 感應 resonance and 類 category instead. Joanna Muchowska, "Investigating the history of folk Taoism: the concept of magic in the study of zhuyou rituals," UR Journal of Humanities and Social Sciences 28:3 (2023), 98-110, argues that sinologists are using "magic" in a way the theoretical literature has moved past, holding magic and religion apart where the theory now treats magic as parareligious, and that the classical definitions need rebuilding before they can be applied to 祝由 at all. Which is a sharper objection than the one I'd have written for her, and it lands on me. And it's also why this chapter doesn't argue the definition. It counts operations and lets you name them, so the objection bruises the label and leaves the ledger standing. G. E. R. Lloyd's Magic, Reason and Experience (Cambridge University Press, 1979) and Stanley Tambiah's Magic, Science, Religion, and the Scope of Rationality (Cambridge University Press, 1990) are the general demolitions of the Frazerian scheme, and Tambiah is what the field reaches for now instead. Richard J. Smith argues the other way, that "magic" is worth keeping as an analytic category for exactly this material, in "Magic Matters: Engaging with Nathan Sivin on the History of Divination, Medicine, and Popular Religion in China," International Journal of Divination and Prognostication 5:1 (2024). Read Muchowska and Smith against each other and you'll have the shape of the argument I'm standing inside. The list is a family resemblance, not a scorecard.

Sources for the contagion cases. The swaddling instruction is Sun Simiao, 備急千金要方, the 少小嬰孺方 pediatric section, 初生出腹 on the newborn; Sabine Wilms has translated that section as Venerating the Root: Sun Simiao's Bei Ji Qian Jin Yao Fang, Volume 5, Pediatrics (Happy Goat Productions, 2013). The 令兒長夀 line is 王燾 Wang Tao's 外臺秘要方, Arcane Essentials from the Imperial Library, 752, juan 35, 小兒初生將護法.

If you want more of them than the chapter uses, the vein is deep and here's where it runs. 本草綱目 juan 38, the 服器部 clothing-and-utensils division, seventy-nine items whose therapeutic claim rests on what the object was in contact with; Paul Unschuld has translated it entire as Ben Cao Gang Mu, Volume VIII (University of California Press, 2021). Entry 38-25, 死人枕席, for 尸疰 corpse-infusion, with the instruction to rebury the pillow, 埋於故處, once the patient recovers; the physician is 徐嗣伯 Xu Sibo, fifth century, and 南史 juan 32 carries his own account. 燒褌散 at 傷寒論 line 392, in Mitchell, Ye and Wiseman's Shang Han Lun: On Cold Damage (Paradigm, 1999). The 禹藏埋胞圖 placenta-burial chart in the Mawangdui 胎產書, in Harper's volume, with Jen-der Lee's "Childbirth in Early Imperial China," in Angela Ki Che Leung, ed., Medicine for Women in Imperial China (Brill, 2006), on the burial rules. The infusion diseases as a disease class, 諸病源候論, the Sui nosology of 610, 卷二十四 juan 24, 注病諸候. And J. J. M. de Groot's The Religious System of China, volume VI, chapter XV, "Sepulchral Medicines and Amulets," which is out of copyright, free, and covers a good deal of this ground in English.

On the word "magical" being usable in the field at all. Michel Strickmann's Chinese Magical Medicine, edited by Bernard Faure (Stanford University Press, 2002). This one, it's the anchor for the whole book. If you chase one source out of this chapter, chase that one. Donald Harper's Early Chinese Medical Literature (Kegan Paul, 1998) defends "magico-religious" as a working category, for the excavated manuscripts, on the ground that the two halves of that word don't name opposite categories in early China.

On the practitioner inside the treatment, and on why a failed method makes practitioners believe harder. Both arguments get their own chapter later, and the sources travel with them. Balint and Kaptchuk on the first. Evans-Pritchard, Horton and Festinger on the second. You'll want them in that order.

On putting pulse and tongue next to physiognomy, which is the most attackable thing in this chapter. Read the people who have worked this ground, including the ones who disagree with me. Elisabeth Hsu, Pulse Diagnosis in Early Chinese Medicine (Cambridge University Press, 2010), is the standard monograph on the subject and you should have it before you take my word for anything. Shigehisa Kuriyama, The Expressiveness of the Body and the Divergence of Greek and Chinese Medicine (Zone Books, 1999), is the book on what Chinese practitioners took themselves to be doing when they looked and touched. On the relation between physiognomy and medicine specifically, Catherine Despeux has worked it in "From prognosis to diagnosis of illness in Tang China: comparison of the Dunhuang manuscript P. 3390 and medical sources," in Vivienne Lo and Christopher Cullen, eds., Medieval Chinese Medicine: The Dunhuang Medical Manuscripts (RoutledgeCurzon, 2005), 176-205, where she puts medical physiognomy inside the wider divinatory art of 相人 xiàngrén, reading a person, and Xing Wang, Physiognomy in Ming China: Fortune and the Body (Brill, Sinica Leidensia 149, 2020), argues that the borrowing ran from medicine into physiognomy rather than the other way, and that the two hold the body apart. That runs against me and you should weigh it. And note the classification problem I flagged further up: the Han imperial bibliography filed 形法, the form-and-model arts that physiognomy sits in, under 數術, while the medical canons sat under 方技. The tradition itself separated them. I'm arguing past that, and this is the second time I've told you so.

On 相 and 象, the two characters I take apart in the divination item above. Every piece of it, so you can check it. The graph 相 as 目 eye plus 木 tree, and the gloss 省視, to inspect closely, are the 說文解字, the Shuowen Jiezi of about 100 CE, which is the standard character dictionary of the tradition and is searchable free at zdic.net. The Shuowen's entry for 象 is the elephant, 長鼻牙，南越大獸, a long-nosed tusked beast, a great animal of the southern Yue lands. The bones passage is 韓非子, the Han Feizi, chapter 解老, Explaining Laozi: 人希見生象也，而得死象之骨，案其圖以想其生也，故諸人之所以意想者皆謂之象也. People rarely see a living elephant, so they get the bones of a dead one and picture the living animal from them, and that's why whatever people build in their minds gets called 象. Two things to hold. The Han Feizi is a philosophical text making a point about Laozi, not a philologist's derivation, and modern scholarship treats it as folk etymology. I use it for what the tradition took itself to be doing. And 相 and 象 are not cognates. Separate words, homophones in modern Mandarin only. Character work here runs on the Shuowen, on Axel Schuessler's ABC Etymological Dictionary of Old Chinese (University of Hawai'i Press, 2007), and on Baxter and Sagart, not on TCM textbooks.

On the gnosis item. The three instructions are Su Wen 25, 寶命全形論, and the ones I paraphrase are 如臨深淵，手如握虎，神無營於眾物. Su Wen 54 glosses them, and the gloss cuts both ways, which is why chapter six rather than this one is where I argue it out. On 存思, interior visualization, Isabelle Robinet is the standard scholar and her work on Shangqing meditation is where to start. I am deliberately not claiming the acupuncture canon prescribes trance; it prescribes a state, and the ecstatic end of the axis belongs to the 巫 and to Daoist adepts, not to the needler. If you catch me sliding those together anywhere, that's an error.

On the talismanic item. 符 fú and 符水 talisman water are covered throughout Michel Strickmann's Chinese Magical Medicine, which is the anchor I keep pointing you at. Sun Simiao's 禁經, rendered Book of Interdiction by Yan Liu and Book of Spellbinding elsewhere, is in the 千金翼方, the sequel to the formulary, and chapter four goes through it properly. For the twentieth-century market: Luis Fernando Bernardi Junqueira, "Revealing Secrets: Talismans, Healthcare and the Market of the Occult in Early Twentieth-century China," Social History of Medicine 34:4 (2021), which is open access through UCL's repository. The distinction I draw at the end of that item, that a talisman makes a link rather than finding one, is mine and it is a reading, not a finding.

On the illumination item. The two chapters are 大醫習業 and 大醫精誠, and they open juan one of Sun Simiao's 備急千金要方, ahead of the formulas. 大醫精誠 is the famous one, usually rendered "On the Absolute Sincerity of Great Physicians," and it is the text people reach for when they want a Chinese medical ethics. I am working from the Chinese and from the standard accounts of the chapter's placement, not from a print edition in my hands. For the long form of the practice, 養生 nourishing life and 內丹 internal alchemy, Fabrizio Pregadio is the standard scholar on the alchemical side and Vivienne Lo has worked the nourishing-life material in the excavated manuscripts. And the claim that the standardized curriculum demoted cultivation from practice to coursework is mine, off my own training and my own teaching, not a finding from the literature.

On the banishing item. 張從正 Zhang Congzheng, styled 子和 Zihe, c. 1156 to 1228. His book is the 《儒門事親》 Rumen shiqin, Confucians Serve Their Parents, and no complete English translation of it has been published, so the two lines I quote are my own renderings off the Chinese, both from 〈汗下吐三法該盡治病詮〉, the essay arguing that sweating, purging and vomiting exhaust the treatment of disease: 夫病之一物，非人身素有之也。或自外而入，或由内而生，皆邪氣也, and 邪去而元氣自復. It's item thirteen in the standard fifteen-juan text, and because the items are numbered straight through the whole book, thirteen falls third inside 卷二, between 攻裏發表寒熱殊塗箋十二 and 凡在上者皆可吐式十四. The received text reads 邪去而元氣自復也 and I've dropped the final 也. On the school label, be careful with me. 攻邪, attacking evil, is what Chinese reference literature keyed to 《中医各家学说》, Doctrines of the Various Schools of Chinese Medicine, calls the current. English-language writing splits: the Journal of Chinese Medicine says Attack and Drain School, the Journal of Traditional Chinese Medical Sciences says school of purgation, and I'm following the second, and Zhang is standardly called its 代表人物, its representative figure, rather than its founder. The larger caution is that the whole "four great masters of the Jin and Yuan" grouping was assembled later: Fabien Simonis, "Illness, Texts, and 'Schools' in Danxi Medicine," in Benjamin A. Elman, ed., Antiquarianism, Language, and Medical Philology (Brill, 2015), traces how the set was built in stages, with Zhang Congzheng substituted into it in the seventeenth century by 張璐. So I use the label the field uses, and you should know it was applied backwards. A short peer-reviewed English note exists: Huang and Liang, "Zhang Congzheng, physician and initiator of the 'school of purgation'," Journal of Traditional Chinese Medical Sciences 5:4 (2018), 321-322.

On expulsion as a category, and who got there before me. Philip S. Cho, "Healing and Ritual Imagination in Chinese Medicine: The Multiple Interpretations of Zhuyou," East Asian Science, Technology, and Medicine 38 (2013), and his 2005 Penn dissertation on zhuyou exorcism, are the closest existing work to this. Yan Liu, "Words, Demons, and Illness: Incantatory Healing in Medieval China," Asian Medicine 14:1 (2019), argues that incantatory healing ran on exorcistic principles inside a state medical institution, which is what makes expulsion institutional rather than folk. Michael Stanley-Baker, "Daoism and Medicine," in the Routledge Handbook of Chinese Medicine (2022), open access, treats expulsion as conceptually fundamental to Daoist healing, and describes the fasting, purification and retreat that precede a Celestial Master healing. What none of them argues, and what I'm extending, is that the ordinary pharmacological and needling repertoire runs the same operation. Take that last step as mine. And the reading that clearing and settling are one move rather than two, that's my synthesis off Zhang's sentence, not something I can hand you out of a journal.
